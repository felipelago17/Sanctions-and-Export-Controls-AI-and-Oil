# Knowledge Inference Pathways

*How enforcement agencies construct knowledge in export control proceedings.*

---

!!! info "Cross-references"
    For the foundational statutory standard, see [High-Probability Knowledge Standard](high-probability-standard.md).
    For inchoate offences that incorporate these pathways, see [Inchoate Provisions Application](inchoate-provisions-application.md).

---

## I. Overview

Export control enforcement rarely begins with a defendant who openly admitted knowledge. BIS and the Department of Justice — National Security Division (DOJ-NSD) instead assemble knowledge from circumstantial evidence chains. Understanding these pathways matters both for evaluating the strength of an enforcement action and for designing compliance programmes that interrupt the pathways before they ripen into agency findings.

There are five principal inference pathways:

| Pathway | Description | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural knowledge** | Organisational position creates presumed awareness | Job title, access to compliance systems, prior training |
| **Inquiry notice** | Red flags triggered a duty to investigate; failure to investigate is itself evidence | Supplement 3 indicators; anomalous transaction characteristics |
| **Actual notice** | BIS correspondence or public agency action placed entity on formal notice | "Is informed" letters (§ 744.22); Entity List additions; warning letters |
| **Corporate attribution** | Employee knowledge attributed to the entity | Agency law; compliance programme design |
| **Pattern-of-conduct** | Prior violations or near-misses demonstrate systemic knowledge | Prior settlements; voluntary self-disclosures; compliance audit findings |

---

## II. Structural Knowledge

Structural knowledge arises from an individual's role and access within an organisation. It is distinct from actual knowledge of a specific transaction; it is the background awareness that a person in a given role is presumed to hold.

**Applications in enforcement:**

- A compliance officer who received EAR training and had access to the entity's screening system is presumed to know the ECCN classifications of the entity's products, even if they did not review each transaction.
- A sales executive who handled contracts with Chinese counterparties is presumed to know the Entity List status of major Chinese AI research entities, given the public availability and widespread industry coverage of such designations.
- A logistics manager who arranged transshipment through third-country intermediaries with no business nexus in the transit jurisdiction is presumed to be aware of the diversion risk.

!!! note "Structural knowledge ≠ strict liability"
    BIS has not taken the position that structural knowledge alone satisfies the § 772.1 standard without corroborating circumstantial evidence. It establishes a baseline that other evidence layers onto. In civil penalty negotiations, the presence of structural knowledge without supporting red flags typically results in mitigation rather than a maximum-penalty finding.

## III. Inquiry Notice (Red Flag Doctrine)

Inquiry notice is the most operationally significant pathway. When a red flag arises — any of the indicators in Supplement 3 to Part 732 — the entity incurs a duty of inquiry. Failure to perform reasonable inquiry, or performing inquiry but ignoring results, supports the inference of high-probability awareness.

### The Supplement 3 Indicators (15 CFR Part 732, Supp. 3)

BIS's "Know Your Customer" guidance identifies the following red flags:

1. **Evasiveness about end-use.** Customer declines to provide information about the intended use of the item or is vague.
2. **Unfamiliarity with the product.** Customer appears unfamiliar with the technical specifications of the item they are purchasing.
3. **Business inconsistency.** The items ordered are inconsistent with the buyer's stated line of business.
4. **No service/installation need.** Customer declines installation, training, or after-sale support for complex technical equipment.
5. **Unusual payment.** Payment in cash, through unusual channels, or by a third party with no stated connection to the transaction.
6. **Unusual shipping.** Request for unusual packaging, over-specification of containers, or routing through atypical transit points.
7. **Inconsistent destination.** The destination country or freight forwarder is inconsistent with the stated end-user's location.
8. **Freight forwarder as end-user.** The listed end-user is a freight forwarder with no operational facility matching the stated use.
9. **Inconsistent specifications.** Technical specifications requested exceed what the stated end-use would require.
10. **Prior concern.** The party has previously triggered compliance holds or has appeared in trade-press reporting about export controls.

### Red Flag Combinations

The presence of any single indicator does not automatically satisfy the inquiry-notice standard. BIS evaluates combinations:

- **Two or more indicators** from Supplement 3 co-present in a single transaction is BIS's informal threshold for a mandatory compliance hold.
- **One strong indicator** (e.g., the buyer is a freight forwarder listed as end-user for advanced AI accelerators destined for an unnamed research facility) may independently satisfy inquiry notice.
- **A single weak indicator** (e.g., unusual payment terms in an otherwise clean transaction) requires additional context before triggering the standard.

### Duty to Investigate and Standard of Inquiry

Once inquiry notice arises, the entity must take reasonable steps proportional to the risk:

- Request and document the end-use/end-user information
- Screen the updated information against all applicable lists
- Obtain an end-user certificate or equivalent assurance for controlled items
- Escalate to legal/compliance for controlled-threshold items

The investigation standard is reasonableness, not absolute certainty. An entity that documents a good-faith investigation — even one that ultimately turns out to be insufficient — is in a materially better position than one that took no steps.

## IV. Actual Notice

Actual notice converts constructive or high-probability knowledge into documented certainty for enforcement purposes. It eliminates many defences available in ambiguous cases.

### "Is Informed" Letters — 15 CFR § 744.22

BIS may issue a letter to a specific exporter notifying it that a particular transaction or series of transactions is prohibited, even if no licence would otherwise be required. Upon receipt:

- A licence is required for any export, reexport, or transfer of items subject to the EAR to the identified party
- The requirement applies regardless of ECCN, including EAR99 items
- The letter creates a permanent record in BIS's enforcement file

The "is informed" mechanism is BIS's most targeted instrument for creating actual notice without requiring a formal Entity List addition. It is used frequently in enforcement investigations to test whether a subject will comply once notified.

### Entity List and MEU List Additions

An entity's appearance on the Entity List (Supplement 4 to Part 744) or Military End-User List (Supplement 7 to Part 744) creates:

- Constructive notice to all exporters upon Federal Register publication
- A licence requirement for all items subject to the EAR (Entity List) or specific items (MEU List) from the effective date
- An inference that any entity that screened and identified the party knew of the control

!!! warning "Constructive notice date matters"
    BIS has taken the position that notice runs from the Federal Register publication date, not from the date a specific exporter actually performs its screening. An exporter that screens monthly and misses a Federal Register publication in an interim period may still be charged with knowledge from the publication date, not from its next screening cycle.

### Prior BIS Warning Letters

Warning letters are formal BIS communications that do not impose penalties but document that BIS has identified a potential compliance issue. Receipt of a warning letter:

- Creates actual notice of the specific concern identified
- Is treated as an aggravating factor in any subsequent enforcement proceeding involving the same conduct
- Eliminates the "first offence" mitigation credit that would otherwise be available

## V. Corporate Attribution

Corporate attribution governs how the knowledge of individual employees is attributed to the corporate entity in enforcement proceedings.

### Standard Attribution Rule

Under general agency principles as applied in export control enforcement:

- Knowledge of an employee acting within the scope of their employment is attributed to the employer
- This applies regardless of whether the employee's knowledge was shared with senior management
- The knowledge of a single compliance officer, sales manager, or freight coordinator can satisfy the knowledge element for the entire corporate entity

### Compartmentalisation Risk

Respondents sometimes argue that the relevant knowledge was siloed in a business unit that operated separately from the corporate compliance function. BIS and DOJ have generally rejected this defence where:

- The compliance function had access to the relevant systems
- The compartmentalised unit was not operating under a separate legal entity
- The corporate entity held itself out as a unified enterprise to the customer

### Successor Liability

In merger and acquisition contexts, the acquiring entity may inherit the predecessor's knowledge base — including prior BIS correspondence, compliance audit findings, and identified risk areas. This is a significant due diligence concern in M&A transactions involving entities with substantial export-controlled business.

## VI. Pattern-of-Conduct Evidence

Pattern-of-conduct evidence is used to establish that a respondent had systemic, not merely transactional, awareness of export control risks.

### Prior Settlements and Administrative Orders

A prior settlement with BIS (charging letter + civil penalty) or a compliance order is treated as:

- Definitive evidence that the respondent was aware of EAR requirements in the covered area
- A baseline against which recidivism is measured — repeated violations in the same area after a prior settlement carry maximum-penalty exposure
- Contextual evidence that senior management was aware of export control risk at the entity level

### Voluntary Self-Disclosures

A prior Voluntary Self-Disclosure (VSD) — even one that was resolved favourably — creates a record of awareness. BIS tracks VSD history; a subsequent violation in an area covered by a prior VSD eliminates the good-faith mitigation credit that VSDs otherwise generate.

### Compliance Audit Findings

Internal compliance audit findings that identified a specific risk area and were not acted upon are among the most damaging pieces of pattern evidence. They establish that:

- The entity was aware of the risk
- The entity made a conscious decision (or consciously failed) to remediate
- The failure to act cannot be characterised as inadvertence

!!! warning "Privilege considerations"
    Compliance audit findings are often created by counsel and may be attorney-client privileged. However, privilege does not protect the underlying facts (only the legal analysis). BIS can obtain privilege-protected communications in criminal proceedings through the crime-fraud exception if there is evidence the entity used the audit to conceal violations rather than to remediate them.

## VII. Anti-Circumvention Indicators

Where a respondent structured transactions to avoid export control obligations, BIS treats the structuring itself as knowledge evidence under the evasion provision at 15 CFR § 764.2(h).

Common anti-circumvention indicators that support knowledge inference:

- Use of a newly formed shell entity as the named exporter or end-user
- Routing through a jurisdiction with no demonstrated commercial nexus to the transaction
- Breaking a single shipment into multiple below-threshold transactions to avoid ECCN classification
- Substituting item descriptions on export documentation
- Using intermediaries who lack plausible business reasons to be in the supply chain

Each of these indicators both supports a knowledge inference under § 772.1 and provides independent grounds for an evasion charge under § 764.2(h).
