# Managing Export Controls Risk in M&A Transactions

*A Practical Manual for Buyers, Sellers, and Deal Teams*

**Based on:** *Red Flags Rising*, Episode 45, "M&A and Export Controls – from Art of War to Art of the Deal" (Michael Huneke, Morgan Lewis & Brent Carlson, Red Flags Rising Solutions), published 10 September 2026, and the authors' related publications.

!!! note "Source note"
    This manual synthesises the published episode description and the hosts' body of written work (NYU PCCE blog series, WorldECR) with established US enforcement precedent. It is not legal advice.

---

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This manual sets out a structured approach to identifying, allocating, and remediating US export controls risk (EAR, ITAR, and OFAC sanctions) across the full M&A lifecycle:

1. Pre-acquisition due diligence
2. Signing-to-closing
3. Post-acquisition integration
4. Seller-side preparation

It reflects the current enforcement environment in which export controls have moved from a technical trade specialty to what the hosts describe as **"central compliance risks"** engaging boards' duty of oversight under Delaware law.

**Who should use it:** deal counsel, corporate development, compliance and trade teams, integration leads, and boards/audit committees of both buyers and sellers.

---

## 2. Why This Matters: The Enforcement Backdrop

**Successor liability is well established.** BIS and OFAC hold acquirers liable for a target's pre-closing violations — even in asset deals, and even where the buyer did not know of the conduct.

| Case | Key principle |
|------|---------------|
| *In re Sigma-Aldrich Bus. Holdings* (BIS ALJ 2002) | Acquirer of asset business held liable for predecessor's unlicensed exports of tetrodotoxin citrate; settled US$1.76m. Foundational EAR successor liability precedent. |
| *Sirchie Acquisition Corp.* (2010) | Maximum civil penalties for acquiring a company that had violated a denial order against its CEO. |
| *C.A. Litzler* (2014) | US$45,000 penalty under successor liability for a single pre-acquisition EAR violation. |
| *Boeing/Hughes* | US$32m settlement notwithstanding the acquired subsidiary's violations had ceased years before the acquisition. |

**Export controls are the "new FCPA."** Enforcement runs on the EAR's "knowledge" standard, which includes awareness of a *high probability* of a violation — conscious disregard and willful blindness count. Diligence gaps can themselves create knowledge.

**Board exposure.** Under *Caremark* as applied in *Marchand v. Barnhill* and *In re McDonald's*, boards must ensure reporting systems exist for mission-critical compliance risks. For companies with controlled products, software, or technology, export controls are mission-critical — a failed acquisition integration is a paradigm oversight failure.

**Geopolitics moves faster than deal timelines.** Entity List additions, the Affiliates Rule, country-group changes, and US–China negotiation cycles can re-price a target between signing and closing. Diligence must be forward-looking, not just historical.

## 3. Analytical Framework: The Fraud Four-Circle Framework℠

Carlson's **Fraud Four-Circle Framework℠** (NYU PCCE, 8 January 2026) directs diligence teams to assess not just what the target's paperwork says but whether the *conditions for evasion or misconduct exist*:

| Element | Deal-diligence question |
|---------|------------------------|
| **Pressure / Incentive** | Does target revenue depend on restricted markets, distributors in transshipment hubs, or customers likely to be listed? Are sales teams commissioned on China/Russia-adjacent flows? |
| **Opportunity** | Are controls weak — no ECP, manual screening, no classification database, fragmented ERP, third-party-heavy routes to market? |
| **Rationalization** | Is there a culture of "everyone ships through Dubai/Hong Kong," "it's the distributor's problem," or "the item is EAR99 anyway"? |
| **Capability (fourth circle)** | Who at the target has the seniority, technical knowledge, and access to structure around controls (entity shifting, mislabelling, splitting shipments)? |

!!! tip "Practical implication"
    Paper diligence (certificates, policies, letters of assurance) is necessary but insufficient. The framework directs interviews and transaction testing at the *people and pressures*, not just the policies. A signed assurance "may not be worth the paper it's written on" if the four-circle conditions are present.

## 4. Phase I — Pre-Acquisition Due Diligence

### 4.1 Scoping and Risk-Tiering

- Determine **US nexus**: US-origin items, US-content above de minimis, US-person involvement, USD clearing, foreign-produced items caught by the Foreign Direct Product rules.
- Classify the target's portfolio at a triage level: ITAR/USML items, CCL-listed ECCNs (especially 3A/3B/4A/5A/9x515/600-series), EAR99.
- **Tier the deal:**

| Tier | Characteristics | Diligence depth |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| **High** | Semiconductors, aerospace/defence, AI/compute, dual-use chemicals/bio, encryption; sales into China, Russia-adjacent corridors, or Gulf transshipment ecosystem | Trade counsel + forensic/analytics resource; not a generalist checklist |
| **Medium** | Some controlled items; limited restricted-market exposure | Enhanced standard diligence |
| **Low** | EAR99 portfolio; no restricted-market sales | Standard review |

### 4.2 Core Document Requests

1. Export compliance programme (ECP) documents, risk assessments, audits, and management review minutes
2. Classification records (ECCN/USML determinations and who made them), CJ/CCATS requests
3. Licences, licence exceptions relied upon, agreements (TAAs/MLAs), and provisos
4. Five years of export transaction data (EEI/AES filings, invoices, airway bills) — sampled against screening logs
5. Restricted-party screening methodology, match-resolution records, and screening vendor coverage (including Entity List, MEU, SDN, 50% Rule, and Affiliates Rule exposure)
6. Distributor/reseller agreements, end-use/end-user statements, and any letters of assurance
7. Prior voluntary self-disclosures, subpoenas (including BIS administrative subpoenas), is-informed letters, and any government correspondence
8. Deemed-export posture: foreign-national employee access to controlled technology; technology control plans
9. Whistleblower/hotline reports touching trade, shipping, or customs

### 4.3 Transaction Testing and Red Flags

Test data against known evasion patterns — do not rely on representations alone:

=== "Transshipment patterns"
    - Sales spikes to transshipment hubs (Hong Kong, UAE, Türkiye, Central Asia, SE Asia) that correlate with restrictions on the ultimate market
    - Post-2022 insertion of third-country intermediaries

=== "Entity shifting"
    - Old customers disappearing and "new" customers with shared addresses, directors, phone numbers, or beneficial owners appearing
    - A signature evasion pattern; see Carlson (2023) on entity-shifting detection

=== "Order anomalies"
    - Orders inconsistent with a customer's line of business
    - Freight-forwarder consignees; reluctance on end-use statements
    - Pricing anomalies or unusual payment routes

=== "Anecdotal channels"
    - Exit interviews, sales-team chatter, and customer-service records often reveal what the data hides
    - Discrepancies between interview accounts and documents are themselves findings

### 4.4 Interviews

Interview the classification owner, head of logistics, top regional sales leads, and the compliance lead — **separately**. Probe the four circles: incentives, override ability, and rationalisations. Discrepancies between interviews and documents are findings.

### 4.5 Valuation and Structuring Consequences

- **Quantify exposure:** penalty ranges (per-violation civil penalties, potential disgorgement, monitorship costs), revenue-at-risk from restricted customers, and remediation cost.
- **Structural tools:** purchase-price adjustment, escrow/holdback, specific indemnities for identified issues, walk-away rights for material findings.
- Consider whether the *seller* should file a voluntary self-disclosure pre-closing — several practitioners recommend this where patterns of non-compliance appear.
- **ITAR deals:** DDTC 60-day advance notification where a foreign person will acquire control of a registered party; plan the timeline. Assess CFIUS interaction where relevant.

## 5. Phase II — Signing to Closing

1. **Interim operating covenants:** target must maintain screening, not enter new restricted-market contracts outside the ordinary course, and notify the buyer of any government contact or newly discovered issue.
2. **Representations and warranties:** compliance with export controls and sanctions; completeness of disclosure of prior violations, disclosures, and government inquiries; accuracy of classifications. Note: R&W insurance frequently excludes or sub-limits trade issues.
3. **Conditions precedent:** required regulatory notifications/approvals; completion of specified remediation items; no new listing of the target or its key customers.
4. **Bring-down diligence:** re-screen the customer and supplier base immediately before closing — the regulatory landscape may have moved since signing.

---

## 6. Phase III — Post-Acquisition Integration (First 180 Days)

1. **Day-1 controls:** extend the buyer's screening, order-hold, and licence-determination processes over the target's order book immediately; freeze shipments to flagged counterparties pending review.
2. **Deep-dive audit:** a post-closing export controls audit validates deal-stage findings. Follow a "trust, but verify" posture — test transactions, don't re-read policies.
3. **Voluntary self-disclosure decisions:** where violations are confirmed, assess VSD to BIS/OFAC/DDTC promptly. Post-acquisition VSDs are viewed favourably and materially mitigate successor liability; delay converts inherited risk into the buyer's own "knowledge" problem.
4. **Harmonise the ECP:** single classification database, unified screening, integrated ERP flags, technology control plans covering the combined workforce, and training targeted at the target's sales and logistics staff.
5. **Board reporting:** integration plan, findings, and remediation status should flow to the audit/compliance committee — this satisfies the *Caremark* duty of oversight if issues later surface.

---

## 7. Seller-Side Playbook

1. **Pre-sale compliance readiness review** (sell-side diligence): find and fix issues before the data room opens; a clean, documented ECP is a valuation asset.
2. **Consider a pre-closing VSD** for identified violations — it caps the issue, preserves deal value, and avoids the buyer discovering it first.
3. **Curate the data room:** classification rationales, screening logs, audit history, and closed-out hotline matters presented affirmatively.
4. **Negotiate indemnity scope realistically:** knowledge qualifiers, survival periods, and baskets calibrated to the actual risk profile rather than boilerplate.

## 8. Governance: The Board's Duty of Oversight

Delaware's *Caremark* line (as applied in *Marchand v. Barnhill* and *In re McDonald's*) requires boards to implement and monitor reporting systems for mission-critical risks. Carlson & Huneke (January 2024) argue sanctions and export controls now sit squarely in that category for exposed companies.

**In the M&A context, the board should see:**

- [ ] Trade-risk tier of the deal
- [ ] Material diligence findings and their price/structure treatment
- [ ] Integration and remediation plan
- [ ] Post-closing audit results

Minutes should reflect actual engagement. Directors of *sellers* have a mirror-image interest: undisclosed violations discovered post-closing generate indemnity claims and, potentially, fraud allegations.

---

## 9. Rapid Diligence Checklist

- [ ] US nexus mapped (items, persons, currency, FDP rules)
- [ ] Portfolio classification triage complete (ITAR / CCL / EAR99)
- [ ] 5-year transaction data obtained and tested against red-flag patterns
- [ ] Entity-shifting analysis on customer master data
- [ ] Screening methodology validated (Entity List, SDN, 50% Rule, MEU, Affiliates Rule)
- [ ] Licences, exceptions, agreements, and provisos reviewed
- [ ] Prior VSDs, subpoenas, is-informed letters disclosed
- [ ] Deemed-export / TCP review complete
- [ ] Key-person interviews done; four-circle assessment documented
- [ ] Exposure quantified; price/indemnity/escrow treatment agreed
- [ ] DDTC 60-day notice / CFIUS interaction assessed (if applicable)
- [ ] Interim covenants and bring-down re-screening in the SPA
- [ ] Day-1 controls and 180-day integration plan drafted
- [ ] VSD decision framework pre-agreed
- [ ] Board reporting pack prepared
