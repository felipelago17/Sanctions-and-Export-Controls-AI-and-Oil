## I. Overview & Strategic Context

China's export control regime has undergone significant systematisation since 2020, evolving
from ad-hoc measures into a comprehensive legal and administrative framework spanning critical
minerals, rare earths, semiconductor materials, and dual-use items. This architecture serves
dual objectives: (1) **mineral security** — resource optimisation and vertical integration;
and (2) **geopolitical leverage** — reciprocal countermeasures to US and allied semiconductor
and technology controls on Chinese entities.

### Key Legislative Foundations

| Instrument | Date | Scope & Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Export Control Law (ECL)** | Oct 2020 / Dec 2020 | Foundational statute; establishes categories of controlled items (munitions, dual-use, civilian) and licensing mechanisms |
| **Unified List of Export-Controlled Items** | Nov / Dec 2024 | Consolidates and harmonises dual-use item controls; implemented 1 December 2024 |
| **Regulations on Export Control of Dual-Use Items** | Nov / Dec 2024 | Dedicated regulation for dual-use items; first full year of enforcement in 2025 |
| **Regulations on Rare Earth Administration** | Various | Governing production quotas, environmental standards, and export licensing |

## II. Critical Minerals Export Controls: 2024–2026

### A. Medium and Heavy Rare Earths (April 2025 Expansion)

In April 2025, China imposed export controls on seven medium and heavy rare earth elements —
**samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium** — across
multiple forms: metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, permanent magnet materials, targets, and
separator technologies.

**Strategic significance:**

- These elements are essential for high-temperature permanent magnets used in wind turbines,
  advanced motors, aerospace applications, and military systems.
- Permanent magnet materials and separation technologies attracted particular international
  attention owing to their civilian-military dual-use character.
- The measure was invoked on national security grounds under the Regulations on Rare Earth
  Administration.

**Suspension and resumption cycle:**

- October 2025: China expanded controls to include rare earth production equipment and raw
  materials, superhard materials, and lithium batteries.
- Late 2025: Controls were **suspended for one year** following the Trump–Xi agreement,
  signalling strategic calibration of export pressure in response to US–China trade
  negotiations.

!!! warning "Suspension does not mean removal"
    The late-2025 suspension of rare earth controls is time-limited and linked to diplomatic
    conditions. Entities that restructured supply chains on the assumption of permanent
    suspension face re-exposure when the suspension lapses or if diplomatic conditions change.
    Monitor MOFCOM (mofcom.gov.cn) for any modification.

### B. Strategic Materials Prohibitions (December 2024 Escalation)

On 3 December 2024, China moved from licensing requirements to **outright prohibitions** for
US-bound exports of:

- **Gallium** — semiconductor substrate material
- **Germanium** — infrared optics, semiconductors
- **Antimony** — metallurgical and defence applications
- **Superhard materials** — industrial diamond, cubic boron nitride

**Enforcement mechanism:** End-use licensing for non-US destinations; explicit ban for US
market access.

### C. Extraterritorial Controls and Legal Architecture

In October 2025, China introduced extraterritorial controls on dual-use items and critical
materials, requiring export licences for transfers to **US military end-users** and
subsequently expanded (January 2026) to **Japanese military end-users**.

**Article 49 mechanism:** The 2024 Dual-Use Items Regulation provides statutory authority
for extraterritorial reach, mirroring the structural logic of the US Foreign Direct Product
Rule (FDPR) — a foreign party that transfers Chinese-origin controlled items to a prohibited
end-user may face Chinese enforcement action regardless of where the transfer occurs.

!!! note "FDPR parallel and divergence"
    The FDPR (15 CFR § 734.9) subjects foreign-produced items to US jurisdiction when they
    incorporate US-origin technology or equipment. China's Article 49 mechanism applies a
    similar extraterritorial logic to Chinese-origin controlled items. Unlike the FDPR, which
    operates automatically when jurisdiction conditions are met, Article 49 application
    appears to depend on MOFCOM licensing decisions and has limited published enforcement
    precedent. See [Extraterritorial Enforcement](extraterritorial-enforcement.md) for the
    FDPR framework.

### D. Implementation Challenges and Opacity

!!! warning "Compliance friction and non-uniform application"
    European importers reported long approval times and lack of clarity in licensing
    determinations (2025 survey). Selective implementation has been observed; the European
    Commission confirmed some companies obtained general licences for rare earths (late 2025),
    but reports suggest non-uniform application across jurisdictions and end-users.
    Licensing decisions lack published criteria, creating significant unpredictability for
    corporate compliance. Unlike the US EAR, where ECCN classifications and licensing policies
    are publicly documented, China's regime relies heavily on administrative discretion.

### E. Graphite Scrutiny (October 2024 Tightening)

In October 2024, China tightened scrutiny of **graphite shipments** (critical for
lithium-ion batteries, semiconductors, and metallurgy), marking a pivot from rare earths
toward broader critical material chokepoints. China controls approximately 80% of global
graphite supply; no close substitutes exist at comparable cost.

## III. Dual-Use Items Regulation and Civilian-Military Convergence

### A. The 2024 Unified List

China's November 2024 Unified List of Export-Controlled Items harmonises controls across
five broad categories:

1. **Semiconductors and semiconductor equipment** — production and testing
2. **Advanced materials** — composites, ceramics, rare earth products
3. **Chemical precursors** — including graphite (tightened October 2024)
4. **Biotechnology items** — dual-use biological equipment
5. **Optics and sensors** — infrared, advanced camera systems

**Key distinction from US controls:** Unlike US controls that target specific performance
metrics (ECCN thresholds based on Total Processing Performance for AI chips), China's regime
emphasises **end-user controls** and categorical designations, reflecting both institutional
capacity constraints and political objectives. There is no direct equivalent of the US
Commerce Control List's ECCN numbering system; Chinese controls operate via categorical lists
and MOFCOM licensing discretion.

## IV. Comparative Analysis: China vs US/EU Export Controls

### A. Symmetry and Signalling

China's export control measures exhibit calculated timing consistent with costly signalling
theory. The measures:

- Signal **technological autonomy** and reduced dependence on Western critical materials;
- Demonstrate willingness to absorb short-term economic costs for long-term strategic
  credibility;
- Calibrate intensity in response to US actions — the suspension following the Trump–Xi
  agreement is the clearest illustration.

### B. Enforcement Architecture Comparison

| Dimension | China | US / EU |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary mechanism** | End-user licensing + categorical controls | Performance thresholds + ECCN classifications |
| **Transparency** | Limited published criteria; administrative discretion | Detailed ECCN framework; published licensing policies |
| **Extraterritorial reach** | Article 49 mechanism (2025); precedent limited | FDPR (15 CFR § 734.9); extensive enforcement history |
| **Multilateral coordination** | Evolving (Russia, Global South selective alignment) | Established allies (Japan, Netherlands, Taiwan, South Korea) |
| **Compliance pathways** | Informal diplomatic channels; precedent-based | Published licence-exception framework (EAR Parts 740–746) |

### C. Compliance and Circumvention Dynamics

**Responses to US controls on Chinese entities:**

- Design-out and design-around strategies (e.g. NVIDIA H800 for Chinese market; reliance on
  export-compliant hardware variants)
- Diversified sourcing and supply chain hedging
- Increased domestic semiconductor R&D and vertical integration (Huawei HiSilicon; SMIC
  advanced process development)

**China's countermeasures via export controls:**

- Exploit rare earth dependency to pressure allied manufacturers
- Create uncertainty in critical material supply chains for Western defence and clean energy
  sectors
- Demonstrate reciprocal cost-imposition capacity to justify domestic political support for
  technology containment

## V. Strategic Sectors Impacted

### A. Rare Earth Permanent Magnets and Wind/EV Energy

The April 2025 rare earth controls directly affect:

- **Wind turbine motors** — global offshore wind expansion depends on dysprosium-terbium
  neodymium magnets
- **EV traction motors** — global automotive electrification supply chains
- **Defence systems** — military aircraft, radar, guided munitions

Estimated 20–40% of global supply chains depend on Chinese rare earth inputs. Alternative
sources (Vietnam, Myanmar, Africa) require multi-year development and carry governance and
conflict risks.

### B. AI Chip Supply Chain

China's extraterritorial controls on dual-use items and critical materials create reciprocal
pressure on:

- **Semiconductor equipment suppliers** (ASML, Lam Research, Applied Materials) that depend
  on materials of Chinese origin
- **Chip design companies** reliant on Chinese demand for R&D funding
- **Battery and materials suppliers** critical to electric vehicles and renewable energy

### C. Defence Industrial Base Vulnerabilities

Chinese control of 50–80% of global supply for rare earth elements, graphite for advanced
batteries, and gallium and germanium for military electronics creates structural vulnerabilities
in US/allied defence production — particularly for next-generation hypersonic systems, missile
guidance, and advanced radar.

## VI. Institutional and Enforcement Framework

### A. Regulatory Bodies

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM)** | Primary export control licensing authority |
| **General Administration of Customs** | Border enforcement and declaration monitoring |
| **National Development & Reform Commission (NDRC)** | Rare earth production quotas |
| **Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MFA)** | Diplomatic coordination and foreign countermeasure designation |

A collaborative law enforcement framework among customs, judicial organs, and MOFCOM, 
announced in 2025, suggests institutional maturation of the enforcement apparatus.

### B. Licensing Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Availability | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **General licence** | Selective — allied nations and neutral states | Non-uniform availability; subject to diplomatic conditions |
| **Individual licence** | Case-by-case for sensitive end-uses | Military, advanced manufacturing; opaque criteria |
| **Blanket prohibition** | US (primary target); US military end-users | Gallium, germanium, antimony, superhard materials |
| **Extraterritorial licence requirement** | US and Japanese military end-users (Jan 2026) | Article 49; limited enforcement precedent |

## VII. Escalation Timeline: US–China Export Control Reciprocity

| Phase | Period | Key Events |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Foundations** | 2020–2022 | Oct 2020: Export Control Law enacted. Oct 2022: US introduces ECCN 3A090/4A090 AI chip controls. China advances rare earth production quotas and environmental consolidation. |
| **Phase 2 — Tightening** | 2023–2024 | Oct 2023: US expands semiconductor equipment controls; strengthens CFIUS review. Dec 2024: China prohibits gallium, germanium, antimony, superhard materials to US. Nov 2024: China's unified dual-use list enters force. |
| **Phase 3 — Extraterritorial** | 2025–early 2026 | Apr 2025: China expands rare earth controls to 7 elements; includes separation technologies. Oct 2025: China introduces Article 49 extraterritorial controls. Late 2025: Suspension for one year (Trump–Xi). Jan 2026: Japan military end-user ban introduced. |
| **Phase 4 — Calibration** | 2026+ | General licences for select EU, ASEAN, Global South entities. Continued securitisation of critical mineral supply chains. Further escalation contingent on Taiwan, SCS, or semiconductor breakthroughs. |

## VIII. Risk Assessment for UAE-Nexus Entities

### A. Critical Mineral Exposure

UAE-based energy, technology, and manufacturing entities that depend on Chinese rare earth
inputs face exposure through:

- Dependence on Chinese rare earth inputs for advanced manufacturing, energy infrastructure
  (wind, solar, EV), and technology applications
- Limited domestic or regional alternatives; reliance on stable Chinese export licensing for
  rare earth sourcing
- UAE's neutral positioning in US–China tech rivalry may enable access to general licences
  not available to more aligned jurisdictions — but this is not guaranteed and may change
  with diplomatic conditions

**Opportunity:** UAE bilateral frameworks with China (UAE–China Comprehensive Strategic
Partnership) may support preferential or general licensing for critical materials. This
advantage should be documented and monitored, not assumed.

### B. Dual-Use Items Compliance

UAE entities importing or transiting Chinese dual-use materials and components should:

- Map supply chains for dual-use materials and components sourced from or through China
- Monitor Article 49 extraterritorial reach; assess whether activities could trigger
  a military end-user presumption under Chinese regulatory criteria
- Assess whether US technology bundled with Chinese dual-use components creates a dual
  licensing obligation (Chinese export permit AND US EAR analysis)

### C. Export Control Cascade Risk

If Chinese controls on critical materials tighten or the suspension lapses:

- Energy transition projects (solar, wind, EV charging infrastructure) may face rare earth
  and battery-material bottlenecks
- Defence and security sector modernisation timelines may extend
- Alternative sourcing from Africa or Southeast Asia may carry geopolitical risk or
  supply-chain governance concerns

## XI. Compliance Recommendations

1. **Map supply chains** for dual-use materials and rare earth dependencies; identify Chinese
   versus alternative sourcing and quantify single-source exposure.

2. **Monitor MOFCOM licensing updates**; establish protocols for assessing whether general
   licence availability extends to your entity's jurisdiction and end-use profile.

3. **Assess Article 49 extraterritorial scope**; clarify whether any activities could attract
   a military end-user presumption under Chinese regulatory criteria.

4. **Track US–China diplomatic developments** for control suspension/resumption cycles;
   integrate into procurement risk calendars.

5. **Engage regional and government partners** on strategic mineral security and energy
   transition supply chain resilience.

6. **Publish internal dual-use screening guidance**; integrate China export control compliance
   into procurement and vendor due diligence workflows.
