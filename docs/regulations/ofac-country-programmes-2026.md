# OFAC Country Programmes — Comprehensive vs Targeted (Sanctions Lawyers, 2026)

**Source:** Dmytro Konovalenko, Sanctions Lawyers (Collegium of International Lawyers LP), *Which Countries Are Sanctioned by OFAC in 2026? Full List* — published 21 March 2026, listed as updated 27 October 2026. <https://sanctionslawyers.net/blog-en/ofac-sanctioned-countries-list-2026/>

**Why it is here:** a compact practitioner overview of the two-tier structure of OFAC country programmes (comprehensive embargo vs targeted/sectoral). Useful as a first-pass orientation alongside the [OFAC 50% Rule](ofac-ubo-50-percent-rule.md) and [Sanctions — Oil & Energy](sanctions-oil-energy.md) pages.

## Two-Tier Structure (per the article)

### Comprehensive / country-wide

| Jurisdiction | Instrument cited | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Iran** | ITSR, 31 C.F.R. Part 560 | Secondary sanctions on non-US parties; a February 2026 settlement of $3.777m is cited as evidence of continuing enforcement |
| **Cuba** | CACR, 31 C.F.R. Part 515; Helms-Burton (LIBERTAD Act) | No easing reported |
| **North Korea** | NKSR, 31 C.F.R. Part 510 | Government and military-linked bodies designated; IT-worker and crypto-laundering actions cited |
| **Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk** | Within the Russia framework | Near-comprehensive regional restrictions |

### Targeted / sectoral

| Jurisdiction | Instrument cited | Targets |
|---|---|---|
| **Russia** | EO 14024 and related orders | Major banks, energy, defence, oligarchs — *not* embargoed country-wide; designation pace described as slower in 2025 |
| **Venezuela** | EO 13884 | PDVSA, government officials; oil licensing under ongoing review |
| **Belarus** | EO 13405 | State enterprises and officials |
| **Myanmar** | EO 14014 | Military and military-linked conglomerates |
| **China** | Sectoral only | Technology exports, Entity List, military-linked firms, Xinjiang-related measures |
| **Other** | — | Nicaragua, Sudan, Somalia, South Sudan, Yemen, Zimbabwe, DRC, Central African Republic; thematic programmes (terrorism, narcotics, proliferation, cyber) apply regardless of country |

!!! warning "Correction — Syria"
    The article lists **Syria (SSR, 31 C.F.R. Part 542)** as comprehensive and "still in place as of early 2026". That is **incorrect**. Executive Order 14312 (30 June 2025) terminated the national emergency and revoked the Syria-specific authorities effective 1 July 2025; OFAC removed the Syrian Sanctions Regulations from the CFR by final rule published 26 August 2025, delisted 518 persons designated solely under the programme, and replaced it with the list-based *Promoting Accountability for Assad and Regional Stabilization Sanctions* (PAARSS) programme. Sanctions on Assad-linked persons, human-rights abusers and others remain, and OFAC can still enforce for pre-1 July 2025 conduct. Sources: [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/insights/legal/us-terminates-comprehensive-sanctions-on-syria-and-eases-trade-restrictions); [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/07/a-new-road-to-damascus); [Federal Register 2025-16324](https://frtracker.app/fr/2025-16324).

## Legal Points Made by the Article

- **Country programme vs SDN List** — geography/nationality-based restrictions and named-person blocking are separate; both must be checked.
- **50% Rule** — entities owned 50% or more by blocked persons are blocked ([detail](ofac-ubo-50-percent-rule.md)).
- **Secondary sanctions** — chiefly Iran, Russia and North Korea; exposure for non-US firms without a US nexus.
- **US-dollar nexus** — USD clearing, US-origin goods or US persons bring non-US parties within OFAC jurisdiction.
- **Strict liability** — the article cites a civil maximum of $377,700 per violation or twice the transaction value (figure unsourced and inflation-adjusted annually; check the current OFAC figure).
- **Voluntary self-disclosure** — can reduce civil penalties by up to 50% under the Enforcement Guidelines.
- **Licensing** — specific licences via SNAP-R, said to take roughly 3–6 months (unsourced); general licences need no application.

## Practical Takeaways (per the article)

1. Determine comprehensive vs targeted status before any transaction.
2. Screen counterparties against the SDN List and other OFAC lists, including ownership, vessels and aircraft.
3. Assess secondary-sanctions exposure for Iran, Russia and North Korea even without a US nexus.
4. Monitor updates — lists change frequently.
5. Plan for licensing lead times with comprehensively sanctioned jurisdictions.
6. Consider prompt voluntary self-disclosure if a violation is found.
