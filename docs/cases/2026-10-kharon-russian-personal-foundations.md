# Russian Personal Foundations as Sanctions Shields — Kesaev, Alekperov, Patrushev and the Control Test (Kharon, October 2026)

Kharon's investigation *Who Owns Sanctioned Oligarchs' Empires Now? A Boom in Russian Trusts Is Hiding the Answer* (The Brief, 8 October 2026; Ryan Bacic, Guillaume Rombaut-Enriquez, Ian Talley) documents the rapid adoption of Russian **personal foundations** (*lichnye fondy*) — a trust-like vehicle introduced in March 2022 — as the preferred structure for moving the holdings of newly sanctioned Russians out of their own names. Kharon counted **877 such foundations as of mid-September 2026**, up from 4 in 2022, with the inflection point being Russia's **August 2024** decision to remove founder-disclosure requirements.

The piece is a case study in the **ownership-vs-control** problem at the heart of the OFAC 50% Rule and the EU "ownership or control" test: the foundations have no disclosed beneficiary, but each is managed by a long-standing lieutenant of the oligarch whose assets it absorbed. The EU General Court's 2 September 2026 judgment in *Kesaev v Council* is the first judicial treatment, and it rejects the structure as evidence of divestment.

> **Source status:** Investigative journalism drawing on Russian corporate records, EU court filings and named expert interviews. Corporate-record findings are Kharon's; the General Court judgment is a primary source and should be consulted directly (case reference below). This entry does not assert that any named individual has breached sanctions law.

## The Vehicle

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Legal form | Non-profit personal foundation under Russian civil law, introduced March 2022 for inheritance and asset-management purposes, with favourable tax treatment |
| Original disclosure | Founders publicly disclosed, as for a charitable non-profit |
| **August 2024 change** | Disclosure requirements relaxed; founders may remain hidden. The same year, US Treasury had begun emphasising trusts and family members as circumvention channels |
| Growth | 4 (2022) → 13 (2023) → 135 (2024) → 365 (2025) → **877** (mid-Sep 2026) — per Kharon analysis and an ALRUD report obtained by Forbes |
| Stated drivers | Tax optimisation, inheritance, repatriation of assets from "unfriendly" jurisdictions (Cyprus, UK); per adviser Alexander Baranov, the top risk the foundations protect against is sanctions |
| Typical pattern | Foundation incorporated shortly before taking over a freshly sanctioned person's holdings; no disclosed beneficiary; **manager is a close business associate** of the oligarch |

The structural insight, from Ruslan Stefanov (Center for the Study of Democracy): *"if you control the board, in whatever way, you control the money within the foundation."*

## The Networks

### Igor Kesaev (Megapolis / Mercury Group)

- Sanctioned by seven Western jurisdictions by 2023; EU designation April 2022 as a "leading businessperson" and major shareholder of the **Degtyarev Plant** (Kalashnikov machine guns). Megapolis is Russia's dominant cigarette distributor and a Philip Morris International partner.
- **December 2023:** *Personal Foundation Mercury*, Moscow-based, no disclosed beneficiary, took majority ownership of **Mercury Retail Group** (Krasnoye & Beloye liquor/convenience chain); later added a real-estate company and, in May 2026, a Black Sea health resort (Sanatoriy im. M.V. Frunze).
- **December 2025:** a second foundation (*Private Foundation SOM*) took over another Kesaev company; **April–July 2026:** a third (*Private Foundation Future*) acquired three more (incl. Iksora, Yulizim).
- All three list the same manager: **Aleksandr Brodyagin** — formerly VP legal affairs at Megapolis, head of the presidential administration of Mercury Group, a Degtyarev Plant director, and VP legal at Dixy.
- Separately, Kesaev's 100% stake in the Russian parent of Italy-based **World Fashion Channel Europe** passed to the channel's sole administrator in May 2022 (a month after EU designation), then to a new personal foundation linked to two other Kesaev associates in December 2025.
- In his EU case Kesaev argued he had "transferred his 40% stake in Megapolis Holding BV to a foundation in December 2023" and was now "only an indirect, passive … minority shareholder".

### Yusuf Alekperov (son of Lukoil founder Vagit Alekperov)

- Designated by OFAC January 2025, with his oilfield-services company **Welltech LLC**.
- Two months later, divested 100% of **EKTO OOO** (holding company; c. RUB 62 bn / USD 737 m of assets per Kommersant) to the newly formed **Personal Fund YVS** — initials matching Yusuf Vagitovich. Manager: EKTO director **Konstantin Sanin**, who also holds small stakes in seven of the underlying companies.
- Direct relevance to the [Lukoil / GL 131K case](2026-11-lukoil-boehly-securities-swap-ofac.md): the Alekperov family's broader structuring sits alongside the Lukoil International divestment in OFAC's Russia energy portfolio.

### Andrey Patrushev (son of former FSB director Nikolai Patrushev)

- Designated by OFAC February 2022 (first day of the war); Arctic energy figure, former Rosneft executive.
- Weeks later sold his 37.8% stake in a geophysical-analysis company to a private foundation managed by a business associate; that foundation now indirectly holds a stake in a **Gazprom offshore oil-and-gas service contractor** that previously listed Patrushev as beneficial owner.

## The Legal Test: *Kesaev v Council* (General Court, T-296/24, 2 September 2026)

Kesaev's first annulment actions (Joined Cases T-290/22 and T-763/22) were dismissed on 4 September 2024 and are under appeal. He then challenged the March 2024 relisting (Council Decision (CFSP) 2024/847) in a fresh action lodged 5 June 2024 — **Case T-296/24**. The General Court dismissed it on 2 September 2026 (**ECLI:EU:T:2026:523**), **upholding the designation**. The judgment also holds (paras 161–163) that the "substantial source of revenue" test under the third limb of criterion (g) is applied to the economic sector as a whole, not to the listed person's own turnover or tax payments.

Key holdings as reported:

1. **Burden of proof** on the applicant to show his situation had meaningfully changed, including that shareholder rights in Mercury Group and Megapolis had passed to "an independent third party" — "the applicant has not provided such evidence".
2. **Interposed structures are not divestment:** "the mere fact of using an intermediate legal structure, such as a personal foundation in this case, is not sufficient to demonstrate that the applicant has relinquished control of the shareholdings managed by that foundation."
3. Each further ground (not a "leading businessperson"; tobacco not a "substantial source of revenue"; discrimination) was dismissed in turn.

Stefanov's pre-judgment assessment — that Western authorities might reasonably *suspect* an oligarch behind a foundation but struggle to *prove* it — was answered procedurally: in a delisting challenge the applicant, not the Council, carries the evidential burden, and opacity therefore counts against the person who created it.

### US position

OFAC's March 2026 guidance on "sham transactions" and sanctions evasion gives the example of a blocked person who "transferred ownership of his private jet to a trust, whose sole beneficiary was his unsanctioned wife, while the oligarch continued to use the jet." Erik Woodhouse (Crowell & Moring; formerly State Department sanctions policy) reads this as OFAC treating the oligarch as retaining an **interest** in the property, which is therefore blocked — regardless of nominal title. His warning for screening functions: businesses that screen only for sanctioned *names* "might not find any risks", while the oligarch "can continue to engage with the trustee and the beneficiaries on what to do with" the assets.

## Doctrinal Significance

1. **Control, not ownership, is the operative question — in both regimes.** The EU test is expressly "owned *or controlled*"; OFAC's 50% Rule is ownership-based on its face, but the "interest in property" concept and the sham-transaction guidance reach retained control. A foundation with no beneficiary and a captive manager fails both. See [OFAC UBO & 50% Rule](../regulations/ofac-ubo-50-percent-rule.md) and the control analysis in the [Lithuanian non-performance cases](2026-10-sanctions-clauses-contract-non-performance.md).

2. **The manager is the fingerprint.** Kharon's method — matching the foundation's listed manager to the oligarch's historic corporate officers — is replicable from Russian registry data and should be a standard step in enhanced due diligence on any Russian counterparty whose ownership passed to a *lichny fond* after 2022.

3. **Disclosure withdrawal as state-enabled evasion.** The August 2024 relaxation converted a transparent inheritance vehicle into an opaque one at precisely the moment Western designations of family members and trusts intensified. This is the regulatory analogue of the Chinese blocking measures discussed in the [CSIS sanctions-decay note](2026-10-csis-sanctions-decay-russia-china.md): the target state changes its own law to lower the cost of substitution.

4. **Burden of proof as the enforcement lever.** *Kesaev* shows that courts need not pierce the foundation; they need only hold that the designated person has not disproved continued control. That shifts the practical outcome from "authorities must prove the sham" to "the oligarch must prove the divestment" — a materially more favourable position for sanctions administrators, at least in delisting litigation.

5. **Energy-sector exposure.** Two of the three named cases (Alekperov, Patrushev) are oil-and-gas; the Patrushev foundation sits in a Gazprom offshore-services contractor. Any Gulf or Asian operator contracting with Russian oilfield-services or geophysical companies should assume a post-2022 ownership transfer to a personal foundation is a red flag requiring manager-level analysis, not a clearance.

---

## Compliance Takeaways

- **Screen structures, not just names.** A Russian counterparty whose owner-of-record became a personal foundation after March 2022 — especially after August 2024 — requires identification of the foundation's manager and that manager's employment history with sanctioned persons.
- **Treat "no disclosed founder" as a control indicator, not a null result.** Under both the EU test and OFAC's interest-in-property analysis, inability to identify the beneficiary of a vehicle that absorbed a designated person's assets is itself grounds to treat the assets as blocked.
- **Document the control conclusion.** *Kesaev* places the burden on the designated person in delisting; in enforcement against a third party, the third party will need to show it asked the control question and reached a reasoned answer.
- **Family and lieutenants are the perimeter.** Sanctions programmes since 2024 designate children (Alekperov, Patrushev) and trusts; the foundations are the next layer. Due diligence should map the oligarch's historic officers and family as potential nominees.
- **Expect divergence in proof standards.** The General Court's burden-shifting applies to the applicant in an annulment action; it does not tell a bank or trader what evidence suffices to *clear* a foundation-owned counterparty. Absent positive evidence of independent control, the conservative position is to treat it as controlled.
