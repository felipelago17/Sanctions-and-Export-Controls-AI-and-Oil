# Mind the Gap — Export Compliance as BIS Turns to Enforcement (CSIS, Nov 2026)

Kate Koren and Kevin Kurland (CSIS Economics Program) with Joshua Massey and Ryan Powers (University of Georgia, Benson-Bertsch Center for International Trade and Security), *Mind the Gap: Keeping Export Compliance Within Reach as BIS Focuses on Enforcement* (9 November 2026). The paper argues that BIS is moving from proactive industry engagement to an enforcement-first posture, and that this moves the burden of day-to-day interpretation onto companies — especially small and medium-sized firms without dedicated compliance staff.

> **Source status:** Secondary analysis (think-tank). Descriptions of BIS practice are as reported by the authors; the paper gives no dollar figures for budgets or penalties. Not legal advice.

---

## Core Argument

The authors treat enforcement as legitimate but say it must be paired with support for industry. Their diagnosis is a widening **knowledge gap**: expectations and penalties are rising while outreach and guidance are thinning.

| Gap driver | What the authors observe |
|---|---|
| **Enforcement-first posture** | Penalties rising; BIS expected to receive a significant budget increase (FY2027 submission), mainly for law-enforcement agents and overseas export control officers |
| **Less predictable rule-making** | Requirements increasingly changed through website postings (e.g. the AI diffusion rule rescission press release) and letters to individual companies (e.g. a 2025 letter action targeting Chinese semiconductor firms) rather than formal regulation |
| **Stale reference material** | Entity List, Unverified List and red-flag guidance not regularly updated, leaving vetting judgement calls to firms; guidance described as dense, outdated or hard to find |
| **No professional baseline** | No universally recognised export-compliance career track or competence standard |

## Recommendations (per the authors)

1. **Consolidate and refresh guidance** — a single repository of BIS and related-agency documents with plain-language summaries; update, without creating new policy. The 2017 *Elements of an Effective Export Compliance Program* and *Don't Let This Happen to You* (last updated 2024) are named for updating, including for AI-enabled screening.
2. **Industry collaboration** — joint best practice on screening and red-flag analysis via webinars, training and hypothetical cases.
3. **Talent pipeline** — export controls in university curricula, accredited certification, and public workforce funding (WIOA-funded Workforce Development Boards) for smaller-firm training.
4. **AI with human oversight** — AI for red-flag detection, policy tracking and document-inconsistency checks, with trained officers reviewing output; governance best practice and collaboration among government, universities, think tanks and frontier AI labs.
5. **Partner communication** — companies should state compliance expectations and risk tolerance more clearly to partners, suppliers and distributors.

## Relevance to This Repository

- **Affiliates Rule readiness.** With the rule suspended to 10 January 2027 and the Entity List slow to update, ownership mapping falls on the firm — see [BIS Affiliates Rule](../regulations/uae/bis-affiliates-rule.md) and [OFAC UBO & 50% Rule](../regulations/ofac-ubo-50-percent-rule.md).
- **Informal rule-making as a monitoring problem.** Changes by web posting or company letter are exactly what the [Regulatory Intelligence](../regulatory-intelligence/index.md) pipeline should flag; Federal Register monitoring alone will miss them.
- **AI-assisted screening.** Consistent with the human-in-the-loop position in the [AAEI guide](../screening/aaei-ai-in-trade-compliance.md) and the [Screening Methodology](../screening/index.md).
- **Enforcement context.** See the [Enforcements](../enforcements/index.md) section for the penalty trend the paper builds on.
