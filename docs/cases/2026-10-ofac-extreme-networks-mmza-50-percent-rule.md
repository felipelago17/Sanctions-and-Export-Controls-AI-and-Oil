# OFAC — Extreme Networks, MMZA and the 50 Percent Rule (Oct 2026)

On 27 October 2026 OFAC announced that **Extreme Networks, Inc.** (North Carolina) agreed to pay **USD 1,529,685** to settle **nine apparent violations** of the Ukraine-/Russia-Related Sanctions Regulations. The sales were to **JSC Moskovskiy Mashinostroitelniy Zavod Avangard (MMZA)**, a Russian missile manufacturer majority-owned by **JSC Concern Almaz-Antey**, which was designated on 16 July 2014. Under the 50 percent rule MMZA was therefore blocked. The case was highlighted by Clif Burns on ExportLawBlog on 5 November 2026 under the title "Company Agrees To $1.5M OFAC Penalty For Not Using Google".

> **Source status:** Summarised from the ExportLawBlog post and corroborating secondary reporting (law-firm and compliance commentary, a trade press summary of the DOJ agreement). I did not read OFAC's enforcement release or the DOJ agreement text directly. Penalty arithmetic and the DOJ terms should be checked against those documents before reliance.

---

## Key Facts

- **Company:** Extreme Networks, Inc. (cloud networking provider)
- **Conduct:** nine apparent violations, exports of networking hardware, software and services to MMZA, September 2018 to March 2022, via a distributor and the company's Russian subsidiary
- **Origin:** in March 2018 the Russian subsidiary acquired customer accounts and contracts from a Russian networking business; one acquired purchase agreement named MMZA as end user
- **Blocking basis:** MMZA majority-owned since 2002 by Almaz-Antey (SDN, 16 July 2014); blocked by operation of the **50 percent rule** (31 C.F.R. Part 589 context, Ukraine-/Russia-Related Sanctions Regulations)
- **OFAC assessment:** egregious, voluntarily self-disclosed; base penalty reported at USD 1,699,650, settlement USD 1,529,685; reported credit of USD 487,861 against the parallel DOJ payment
- **Parallel DOJ action:** non-prosecution agreement with a reported criminal penalty of USD 975,721.84, described in commentary as the first "near miss" outcome under DOJ's corporate enforcement policy
- **Total reported:** about USD 2.5 million across both resolutions

## What OFAC Says Went Wrong

- The U.S.-led compliance team reviewed the March 2018 purchase agreement naming MMZA but did not recognise that MMZA was blocked
- Red flags reported in commentary: MMZA email addresses and addresses in purchase orders, a distributor that supplied MMZA's website before listing a different named end user, and customer-portal registrations under the wrong company name
- The company had a policy to review Russia-related dealings; the failure was in applying it to ownership
- OFAC's release gave no detailed guidance on what sufficient due diligence would have required; ExportLawBlog argues a basic web search of the counterparty would quickly have shown the blocked parent

## Why It Matters

1. **Ownership screening, not name screening.** Checking whether the end user itself is on the SDN List is not enough; the 50 percent rule requires ownership analysis of every counterparty, including end users named in acquired contracts.
2. **Acquired books of business.** The exposure came from contracts bought in a 2018 acquisition. Post-deal integration should re-screen every inherited customer, distributor and end user.
3. **Distributor and end-user chains.** A distributor substituting a differently named end user is a classic red flag that should trigger re-verification, not acceptance of the new name.
4. **Self-disclosure matters, but not enough.** Voluntary disclosure halved the base penalty, while the "egregious" finding still applied.
5. **Parallel criminal exposure.** The same conduct produced a DOJ resolution; for multi-agency matters, coordinate disclosures and note the credit mechanism between OFAC and DOJ payments.
6. **Local-subsidiary risk.** The Russian subsidiary's employees handled the MMZA relationship; head-office controls must reach local sales and contract-intake teams.

## Practical Questions for Compliance Teams

- Do we screen to the ultimate beneficial owner for end users named in contracts, and re-screen when ownership data changes?
- Do we re-verify end users when a distributor changes the named customer?
- Were customer contracts acquired in any past deal ever re-screened after closing?
- Do local subsidiaries in high-risk jurisdictions have independent screening and escalation, not just a head-office policy?
