# CJEU — Advocate General Ćapeta on the Meaning of "Dual-Use Items" (Case C-538/25 *Flugzeugherstellerin*, Opinion of 24 September 2026)

On 24 September 2026 Advocate General Tamara Ćapeta delivered her Opinion in **Case C-538/25**, a reference from the Austrian Federal Administrative Court on how to decide whether an item is a "dual-use item" under **Regulation (EU) 2021/821** (the Dual-Use Regulation). It is the first time the Court of Justice has been asked to define the concept directly, and the Opinion proposes a **broad, capability-based test** that would materially widen the scope of national export-authorisation requirements and prohibitions across the EU.

> **Status:** Advocate General's Opinion — not binding on the Court. Judgment pending. The case name is a fictitious one assigned by the Court. Source: CJEU Press Release No. 134/26 (unofficial document for media use); the full Opinion is on CURIA.

---

## Facts

- In **2019** an Austrian manufacturer exported a two-seat civilian aircraft designed for basic pilot training, with related items and technology, to the **Myanmar Air Force**. The Austrian authorities authorised the export.
- In **2023** the company sought to export the same items to the same end user. This time the authorities refused: the items were "dual-use" because they could be used for reconnaissance flights and to train military pilots, and such use could be connected with human-rights violations. Export was prohibited.
- The company challenged the refusal before the Bundesverwaltungsgericht, which referred to the Court the question of the criteria for determining when an item is "dual-use" within the meaning of the Regulation — the gateway to Member States' power to prohibit or condition exports of items that are *not* on Annex I.

---

## What the Advocate General Proposes

| Issue | AG's position |
|---|---|
| **Breadth of the concept** | "Dual-use items" should be interpreted **broadly**, so that competent authorities can adapt to evolving strategic and security considerations of the Member States and the Union. |
| **Free-trade tension** | A broad concept may sit uneasily with the principle of free trade, but it does not give Member States unfettered discretion: the breadth is counterbalanced by the **limited scenarios** in which the Regulation lets them restrict exports of dual-use items — one of which is where export risks leading to **human-rights violations**. |
| **The test** | Assess the item's **physical and technical characteristics at the time of export** to determine its potential usage capabilities. |
| **What is *not* part of the test** | The military capabilities of the end user, the situation in the destination country, the end user's ability to repurpose the item, and the risk of diversion are **not** relevant to whether the item is dual-use. They may be taken into account at the **next** stage — whether an authorisation should be required or refused. |
| **Effective judicial protection** | National authorities must **explain** why, in the particular context, they consider the items dual-use, and — if they prohibit export — why those items raise security or human-rights concerns. |

The structure is two-step: (1) is the item dual-use, judged on capability; (2) if so, should its export be controlled or prohibited, judged on end user, destination and risk. Conflating the two was the error the AG attributes to a narrower reading.

---

## Why It Matters

1. **Catch-all controls become more usable.** Articles 4–10 of Regulation 2021/821 (end-use controls, national measures, human-rights grounds under Art. 5 and Art. 9) operate on items that are "dual-use" but not listed. A capability-based definition means almost any technically capable civilian item — trainer aircraft, drones, imaging equipment, software — can be brought within national control when the end user or destination warrants it. The listing in Annex I stops being the practical boundary of the regime.
2. **Divergence risk across Member States increases.** Because the second step (whether to restrict) is national, a broad first step gives 27 authorities wider room to reach different conclusions on identical items. The AG's reasoning-duty is the counterweight, and it will matter in litigation.
3. **Consistency with EU sanctions practice.** The approach mirrors how the Russia regime treats "items which might contribute to military and technological enhancement" — capability first, destination second. A judgment in these terms would harmonise the dual-use and sanctions logics.
4. **Same end user, different answer.** The 2019 authorisation and the 2023 refusal involved identical items and end user. The AG's framework accommodates that: the item's dual-use character was constant; what changed was the second-step assessment. Exporters should expect authorisations not to be precedents.

---

## Relevance for UAE and Non-EU Exporters

- **EU-origin items re-exported from the UAE.** Where EU exporters supply UAE distributors, the EU exporter's authorisation analysis now has to consider the UAE customer's *onward* destinations at the second step; expect more end-use statements, re-export undertakings and refusals for Gulf intermediaries serving sensitive end users.
- **Civil aviation and training equipment.** Trainer aircraft, simulators, avionics and related technology for military or para-military customers in the region are the direct analogue to the facts; the AG's view that pilot-training capability suffices for dual-use status is the point to note.
- **Comparison with the EAR.** US law reaches the same result by different means — EAR99 items are controlled on end-use/end-user grounds under Part 744 without any "dual-use" threshold question. The EU route still turns on the definition, which is why this case matters.
