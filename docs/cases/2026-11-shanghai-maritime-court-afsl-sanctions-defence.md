# Shanghai Maritime Court: Foreign Sanctions Are No Defence to a Shipping Contract Breach (AFSL Article 12)

The **Shanghai Maritime Court** held that a Singapore carrier could not rely on US sanctions exposure to refuse to perform a contract of carriage for a Hong Kong shipper. The **Supreme People's Court** published the case on **24 June 2026** among six representative maritime cases of 2025, and commentators describe it as the **first Chinese judgment confirming the mandatory application of Article 12 of the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law (AFSL)**. For anyone relying on sanctions clauses in charterparties, bills of lading or supply contracts with Chinese counterparties, it turns an assumed safe harbour into a litigation risk, and Norton Rose Fulbright's commentary stresses that yielding to the Chinese regime is **not** a safe answer either.

| Reasoning | The carrier committed a **fundamental breach** by refusing delivery and returning the cargo; its conduct amounted to **assisting in implementing** discriminatory foreign restrictive measures against a Chinese enterprise (Article 12), which cannot excuse or mitigate contractual liability | The court's own formulation, quoted in the article: foreign unilateral sanctions "cannot serve as a defense" for refusing performance |

### The wider Chinese counter-sanctions context (per Norton Rose Fulbright)

The AFSL was enacted in June 2021 but the conflict with Western regimes "largely remained theoretical" until **2026**, when the State Council promulgated significant regulations in **April** and, since **May**, China issued various orders **prohibiting compliance** with certain measures and investigations imposed or initiated by foreign jurisdictions such as the US and EU. Private civil claims by Chinese parties against foreign counterparties are an additional channel. (One other commentary lists decrees of 31 March and 13 April 2026; those numbers are **not verified** here.) See the [Freshfields analysis of EU FSR and China's blocking orders](2026-11-freshfields-fsr-china-blocking-orders.md) and the [China export-control architecture](../regulations/china/china-export-control-architecture.md).

## What Norton Rose Fulbright says businesses should take from it

1. **Broad reading.** The court read Article 12 broadly. On its reasoning, US unilateral sanctions targeting Chinese entities will likely be characterised as "discriminatory restrictive measures", and suspending or refusing performance to comply with them is likely to be "assisting in implementation". Because neither party was mainland-based, the reach is wide.
2. **Two regimes to assess.** It is no longer enough to analyse liability under the Western regime alone; consequences under China's counter-sanctions regime, including civil claims, must be weighed.
3. **Review contracts.** Look at sanctions clauses (traditionally seen as protective), illegality and force majeure clauses, dispute-resolution clauses, and the bases on which a transaction can be terminated.
4. **Document the decision.** Responses to sanctions developments should rest on a robust, documented risk assessment, distinguishing **legally binding sanctions prohibitions, export-control restrictions or licensing requirements** from **heightened compliance risk or internal policy alone**; genuine conflicts are a **governance matter needing senior sign-off and advice in both jurisdictions**.
5. **Do not assume yielding to China is safe.** OFAC and BIS do not recognise foreign blocking or counter-sanctions laws as a defence to a US violation; conduct structured to satisfy the AFSL at the expense of US compliance can be an **aggravating factor** under OFAC's Enforcement Guidelines (31 C.F.R. Part 501, App. A); a clause undertaking **not to comply with US law** may itself be characterised as evasion or prohibited facilitation.
6. **More defensible drafting (the firm's examples):** condition performance on obtaining any required licence or authorisation; provide for **suspension during a licence application** instead of an immediate right to abandon; structure performance through entities without the relevant jurisdictional nexus where lawful; treat the **arbitral seat and likely enforcement forum** as substantive risk allocation, not boilerplate.
7. **Terminating for sanctions reasons is no longer straightforward;** review the full facts of each transaction before choosing an approach.

## Why it matters for sanctions and shipping compliance

- **The Entity List is an export-control listing, not a blanket prohibition on dealing.** In this case an Entity List designation was the stated trigger for refusal. The article's emphasis on distinguishing binding prohibitions from risk appetite maps directly onto this: check what the listing actually restricts before refusing.
- **Maritime exposure runs through Chinese courts.** The injunction here compelled a carrier to act. Carriers, charterers and agents with Chinese port calls, Chinese affiliates or assets should assume Chinese courts can reach them (inference from the reported facts).
- **Two-sided conflict.** US enforcement exposure and AFSL liability can point in opposite directions; the article treats this as a governance decision, not a default to either side.

### Relevance to UAE and Gulf compliance functions

- UAE shipping and logistics groups with **Chinese counterparties, agents or port calls** should review sanctions, termination, force-majeure and dispute-resolution clauses, and who decides on sanctions-based refusals.
- Add a **"refusal-risk" step** to maritime sanctions work (vessel and counterparty screening, STS and cargo checks): a compliant answer under US, EU, UK or UAE rules may carry liability under Chinese law.
- Prefer **licence-conditional and suspension-based** wording to outright abandonment rights, and record the basis of any refusal.
