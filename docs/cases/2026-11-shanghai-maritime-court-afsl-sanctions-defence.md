# Shanghai Maritime Court: Foreign Sanctions Are No Defence to a Shipping Contract Breach (AFSL Article 12)

The **Shanghai Maritime Court** held that a Singapore carrier could not rely on fear of US sanctions exposure to refuse to perform a contract of carriage for a Hong Kong shipper. The **Supreme People's Court** published the case on **24 June 2026** among six representative maritime cases of 2025, and commentators describe it as the **first Chinese judgment applying the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law (AFSL)** as an overriding mandatory provision. For anyone using sanctions clauses in charterparties, bills of lading or supply contracts with Chinese counterparties, it turns an assumed safe harbour into a litigation risk.

## The case (as reported)

| Item | Reported content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Parties | A Hong Kong shipper (plaintiff) and a Singapore shipping company acting as carrier; the carrier's Chinese affiliate was also a defendant | Identities are not disclosed in the published case |
| Goods and route | Electronic goods worth about **RMB 4.99 million**, shipped from Shanghai to Manzanillo, Panama (arranged October 2022, per HSF Kramer) | |
| Sanctions trigger | After loading, the carrier refused to issue a bill of lading and refused delivery, citing the shipper's placement on a foreign sanctions list | Neither firm identifies the specific US measure; Squire Patton Boggs says public information suggests the shipper may have been on the BIS Entity List and the Section 1260H list, and that neither listing would necessarily have barred the carrier from dealing with it |
| Procedure | The shipper obtained a **maritime injunction** ordering issue of the bill of lading; the carrier issued it, but while the injunction proceedings were pending shipped the goods **back to Shanghai** | The shipper then sued for the cargo's value plus interest; the carrier counterclaimed for container detention charges, which was dismissed (HSF Kramer) |
| Applicable law | HSF Kramer: the carrier relied on standard-form bill-of-lading terms choosing Singapore law; the court found them not properly incorporated and Chinese law most closely connected. Squire Patton Boggs: governing law not stated | **Discrepancy**; both agree the AFSL applied as mandatory law whatever the choice of law |
| Reasoning | The AFSL is an **overriding mandatory provision** of Chinese law under the Law on the Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations (Art. 4); **Article 12** bars implementing, or assisting in implementing, discriminatory foreign restrictive measures against Chinese persons; the carrier's precautionary concern about US penalties was enough, even though it was not enforcing a foreign legal order | HSF Kramer reads this as a broad application of Article 12 |
| Outcome | Judgment for the shipper for the **full cargo value (about RMB 4.99–5 million) plus interest**; sanctions concerns rejected as a defence that could excuse or reduce liability; **no appeal**, so the judgment is final | One outlet converts the sum to about US$700,000; another widely misreports the direction of the ruling and is not relied on |
| Precedent value | Representative cases are not binding in the common-law sense but are highly influential and generally followed by lower courts (Squire Patton Boggs) | |
| Earlier use of the AFSL | A 2025 Nanjing Maritime Court matter invoked the AFSL and settled (Squire Patton Boggs); one commentary describes a vessel arrest and mediated settlement | Not verified |

## Why it matters for sanctions and shipping compliance

1. **Sanctions clauses may not protect a refusal in a Chinese forum.** Commentators say a party may be unable to rely on a clause to excuse non-performance or termination where doing so assists foreign sanctions against a Chinese counterparty, and that a foreign governing-law clause may not exclude the AFSL.
2. **Over-compliance is the real exposure.** Squire Patton Boggs notes the carrier may have over-complied because the listings may not have prohibited the dealings; Chinese courts examine good faith and whether reasonable options to perform were exhausted. A refusal that goes beyond what the sanctions rule actually requires is the weakest position.
3. **Two-sided conflict.** US secondary-sanctions exposure and AFSL liability can point in opposite directions. The case shows a court choosing the second.
4. **Practical reach is through Chinese courts and assets.** The injunction in this case compelled a carrier to act; carriers, charterers and agents with Chinese-port calls, Chinese affiliates or assets should assume Chinese courts can reach them. That is an inference from the facts reported, not a statement in the sources.
5. **Documentation of the decision.** Where a refusal is made on sanctions grounds, the reasoning should rest on an identified legal prohibition, be recorded, and consider alternatives to non-performance (licences, wind-down, escalation). This is general good practice drawn from the courts' reported focus on good faith, not a rule stated in the sources.
6. **Context.** One commentary lists Chinese countermeasure instruments adopted in spring 2026 (decrees of 31 March and 13 April 2026) alongside the AFSL and the Unreliable Entity List; this has **not** been verified here. See the [Freshfields analysis of EU FSR and China's blocking orders](2026-11-freshfields-fsr-china-blocking-orders.md) and the [China export-control architecture](../regulations/china/china-export-control-architecture.md).

### Relevance to UAE and Gulf compliance functions

- UAE shipping and logistics groups with **Chinese counterparties, agents or port calls** should review how sanctions clauses, termination rights and decision records are written, and who decides on sanctions-based refusals.
- Where a US listing is the trigger, check whether it **actually prohibits** the dealing before refusing; the Entity List and the 1260H list have different legal effects.
- Maritime sanctions work (vessel and counterparty screening, STS and cargo checks) should add a **"refusal-risk" step**: the compliant answer under US, EU, UK or UAE rules may expose the company under Chinese law.
