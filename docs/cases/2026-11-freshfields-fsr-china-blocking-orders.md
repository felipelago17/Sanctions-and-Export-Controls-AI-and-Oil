# EU FSR Investigations Meet China's Blocking Orders — Companies Caught Between Brussels and Beijing (Freshfields, Nov 2026)

A Freshfields transactions-blog analysis (9 November 2026; Hazel Yin, Laurent Bougard, Ilka Oberländer, Isabel Chaves, Kryštof Žáček) describes a **conflict-of-laws squeeze**: the European Commission's investigative powers under the **Foreign Subsidies Regulation (FSR)** now collide with **blocking orders** issued by China under its April 2026 *Regulation Countering Unlawful Extra-Territorial Jurisdiction*. Companies holding China-located information can be told by Brussels to produce it and by Beijing not to.

> **Why it belongs in this repository:** it is the mirror image of the US pattern tracked elsewhere here. The EAR/OFAC extend US jurisdiction outward; the FSR does the same for subsidy review; China answers with a blocking regime. The compliance problem is identical — a multinational cannot satisfy two sovereigns at once — and the same risk mapping applies to UAE-nexus groups with Chinese lenders, investors or subsidiaries.
>
> **Status:** Commentary on live matters. Merits judgments in *Nuctech* and *Goldwind* pending; no enforcement of the Chinese Regulation reported as of the article's date. Source: Freshfields blog (secondary source); verify against EU court and PRC primary texts before reliance.

---

## The Collision in One Table

| | **Brussels (FSR)** | **Beijing (Counter-Extraterritoriality Regulation)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Reach** | Foreign subsidies ("foreign financial contributions") to companies active in the EU, wherever headquartered | Foreign measures seen as harming PRC sovereignty, security, development interests or the lawful rights of Chinese persons |
| **Information tool** | Requests for "all necessary information" to companies and third parties; inspections in the EU and, subject to the non-objection of the third state, abroad, including data on servers outside the EU | Blocking orders (Ministry of Justice) barring compliance with, or assistance in implementing, the foreign measure |
| **Sanction for non-cooperation** | Fines up to 1% of aggregate turnover; periodic penalties up to 5% of average daily turnover per working day; decision on **"facts available"** | Investigation and prohibition orders; "Malicious Entity List" (Art. 8); private suits for injunctions and damages by affected Chinese parties (Arts. 12–13); criminal exposure for serious breaches |
| **Escape valve** | Duty to justify each claimed legal restriction | Narrow: application to the State Council legal-affairs department for permission to comply in "special circumstances" |

## Facts and Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **Apr 2024** | Commission dawn raids at Nuctech (security scanners) in Poland and the Netherlands — first ex officio in-depth FSR investigation; Chinese-hosted emails sought |
| **Aug 2024** | General Court dismisses Nuctech's interim application (T-284/24 R): no showing that the data contained state secrets or that Chinese authorisation had been sought and refused; effective investigation requires some extraterritorial access |
| **Jan 2025** | MOFCOM classifies the Commission's FSR practice as a trade barrier |
| **Mar 2025** | ECJ upholds the order on appeal (C-720/24 P(R)) for lack of urgency; merits (T-284/24) pending |
| **Apr 2026** | PRC State Council adopts the Regulation Countering Unlawful Extra-Territorial Jurisdiction |
| **May 2026** | **First blocking order**, in the Nuctech investigation; JD.com / Ceconomy in-depth M&A investigation opened |
| **Jul 2026** | General Court rejects Goldwind's interim application (T-335/26 R), 20 July — no urgency; Goldwind in-depth investigation had opened in Feb 2026 |
| **c. Aug 2026** | **Second blocking order**, in the JD.com investigation; ministry spokesperson accuses the Commission of arbitrary, excessive information requests |
| **Nov 2026** | JD.com / Ceconomy conditionally cleared |

Other FSR matters in the article: Temu (dawn raids at Irish premises, Dec 2025, leading to the first Statement of Grounds over alleged obstruction of inspection); public-procurement in-depth cases against CRRC, Shanghai Electric and LONGi; and **Metro Lisbon (FSP.103117)**, decided wholly on facts available. Every in-depth ex officio investigation to date has involved a Chinese company.

## Who Is Caught

The blocking order is not confined to the investigated company. According to the article it can reach:

- Chinese parents and group entities holding the requested information;
- Chinese banks whose loans are being reviewed as foreign financial contributions;
- managers and employees asked for documents stored in China;
- Chinese third parties — competitors, customers, suppliers — that receive information requests.

**EU companies are exposed too** where they receive China-attributable financing (for example, loans from Chinese state-owned banks) and the Commission seeks information from their Chinese subsidiaries or lenders.

## Analysis

1. **The open factual question is technical versus legal.** The courts have so far treated Nuctech's data-access difficulty as unproven rather than as a genuine legal bar; hearings in April 2026 focused on whether the obstacle was IT architecture or Chinese law. Companies should not assume that invoking Chinese law is a defence — it must be shown specifically, with documented efforts to obtain permission.
2. **Facts available shifts the burden.** If Chinese-held information cannot be produced, the Commission can decide on the facts it has, usually against the company. *Metro Lisbon* shows the tool in use, and the Commission has relied on the *Nuctech* order to dismiss similar arguments.
3. **Blocking orders are not retroactive.** Work done and information collected before an order issues is not caught — a timing argument for front-loading.
4. **Enforcement of the Chinese side is untested.** No sanction under the Regulation has been reported; the first real test will be a company that complies with Brussels and is then listed, sued or investigated in China.
5. **Remedies and timing.** The JD.com / Ceconomy clearance suggests commitments may carry more weight in notified deals where information gaps persist.

---

## Practical Takeaways (adapted from the article)

- **Map early:** identify material Chinese-origin financing and state-linked investors or lenders, locate the underlying information, and test it against PRC data-security and state-secrecy rules.
- **Front-load the FSR workstream** in any transaction with Chinese financing or ownership.
- **Draft for the gap:** cooperation and information-sharing covenants, efforts obligations, warranties and timing/remedy allocation in JV, SPA and financing documents.
- **Build the evidentiary record from lawfully producible material:** EU-accessible information, public Chinese sources, evidence of limited intra-group transfers.
- **Document every restriction claimed** and every permission route pursued.
- **Monitor** the *Nuctech* and *Goldwind* merits rulings and any further blocking orders.

## Relevance for UAE-Nexus and Energy/AI Groups

- **Same structure as the BIS Affiliates Rule problem.** A group with Chinese-held data or Chinese-owned affiliates that is subject to a foreign regulator's information demand needs a documented protocol for *which* sovereign's request is answered, by whom, and on what legal advice — before the request arrives. See [BIS Affiliates Rule](../regulations/uae/bis-affiliates-rule.md).
- **Joint ventures with Chinese partners.** Information-sharing and cooperation clauses should anticipate that the JV partner may be legally barred from supplying documents; allocate the consequence (delay, facts-available outcome, remedies) contractually.
- **Gulf investment into the EU.** State-linked funds and sovereign-backed acquirers are the natural FSR subjects; the Chinese blocking regime shows how a third state can respond when its entities are the targets. Expect the same questions for other state-capital investors.
- **Data localisation overlap.** PRC data-security and state-secrecy law operates alongside UAE and other localisation regimes; cross-border production of documents for any regulator needs a lawful-transfer analysis, not just a privilege review.
