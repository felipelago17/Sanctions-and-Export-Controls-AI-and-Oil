# Analysis — EU Policy Engagement on Advanced Conventional Weapons and Sanctions Compliance in the South Caucasus (EUNPDC Paper No. 101, August 2026)

**Source:** Goguadze, G., *EU Policy Engagement on Advanced Conventional Weapons and Sanctions Compliance in the South Caucasus*, EU Non-Proliferation and Disarmament Consortium, Non-Proliferation and Disarmament Papers No. 101, August 2026 (16 pp.). Author is Director of the Center for Strategy and Development (Tbilisi) and has run US State Department-funded export-control and ACW non-proliferation programmes in the region.

**Why it is in this repository:** the paper is the clearest recent statement of how EU export-control and sanctions expectations reach **third-country** operators that are not legally bound by EU law — through the Article 12g "no re-export to Russia" clause, the 11th-package anti-circumvention tools and the criminalisation directive — and it documents the re-export data that triggered enforcement attention on Armenia and Azerbaijan. The mechanism is identical to the one that reaches UAE operators. The Azerbaijan section (SOCAR, Azerbaijani-flag tankers, Russian crude feedstock at a Turkish refinery) is directly relevant to shipping and energy compliance.

---

## Core Argument

Sanctions compliance and controls on advanced-conventional-weapons (ACW) components should be treated as **confidence-building measures** in the fragile Armenia–Azerbaijan normalisation that followed the US-facilitated August 2025 Washington summit (peace treaty initialled, not yet signed or ratified). The causal chain is indirect — transparency, institutional alignment and reputational signalling — but it operates through institutions and market behaviour rather than through the contested political track, which is precisely what makes it usable while the political track is stuck.

The paper is careful on the legal point that matters for this repository: **Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia are not bound by EU sanctions** and are not Wassenaar participating states. Their alignment is voluntary, so EU engagement has to be framed as incentive-based and cooperative, not as enforcement of an obligation.

## How EU Expectations Reach Non-EU Operators

The paper sets out the three channels through which EU compliance obligations become, in practice, obligations of companies outside the EU. This is the part worth lifting for any third-country compliance programme.

| Channel | Instrument | Effect on a non-EU counterparty |
|---|---|---|
| Contractual | **Art. 12g, Reg. 833/2014** (12th package, Dec 2023): EU exporters must insert a "no re-export to Russia" prohibition for in-scope goods in every contract with a non-EU counterpart; mandatory for contracts concluded on/after 19 Dec 2023 as of 20 Mar 2024 | The non-EU importer takes on a contractual re-export ban enforceable by the EU exporter, with audit/information rights and termination remedies — EU scope is exported by private law |
| Anti-circumvention | 11th package (Reg. 2023/1214, June 2023) anti-circumvention tools; EU Special Envoy for Sanctions Implementation (O'Sullivan, since Jan 2023) with a third-country mandate | Jurisdictions on corridors become subjects of EU diplomatic engagement and listing risk (Art. 12f-style country tools, entity listings in Annex IV) |
| Criminalisation | **Directive (EU) 2024/1226** — common definition of criminal offences and penalties for violating Union restrictive measures | Raises the stakes for EU exporters, which flows down into harder diligence demands on their non-EU customers |

Layered on top: US secondary-sanctions exposure (extraterritorial by design) and the counterparty's own national export-control law. The paper's phrase for the result is a "meaningful expansion of the compliance burden facing third-country actors" — operators in manufacturing, trade, logistics and finance in transit jurisdictions sit under overlapping EU-contractual, US-extraterritorial and national obligations simultaneously.

The author also notes that the **Common High Priority Items List** (EU/US/UK/Japan) has become the operative control list for Russia-related risk — calibrated to Russian military-industrial need rather than to Wassenaar categories — and that EU private-sector guidance in the region should be aligned with it.

## The Data — Why the Region Attracted Enforcement Attention

Figures as cited in the paper (sources noted; several rest on press analyses of trade data):

- **Armenia — microchips and electronics (2022):** imports of microchips up **515 %** from the US and **212 %** from the EU in 2022; exports of integrated circuits to Russia rose from under USD 2,000 to approximately **USD 13 million**; exports of electrical machinery and equipment grew roughly **38-fold** (NYT, Apr 2023; WSJ, May 2023; TradeMap data).
- **Armenia — re-export share of total exports:** ~6 % (2021) → ~26 % (2022) → ~48 % (2023) (Amberd Research Center via Caucasus Watch, May 2025). Armenia's EAEU membership sustains the structural trade channel to Russia.
- **Azerbaijan — UK exports:** approximately **GBP 273 million (~USD 340 million) in 2023**, an **1,860 %** increase on the 2017–21 pre-invasion average (Sky News analysis of UK trade data, cited by Ankura, July 2024).
- **Azerbaijan — energy rather than goods:** in 2025 the EU sanctioned **three Azerbaijani-flagged tankers** affiliated with the state shipping company for Russia trade; the **SOCAR-operated STAR refinery in Türkiye** was reported to run on ~98 % Russian-origin crude feedstock, ~73 % sourced directly, with ~87 % of seaborne crude receipts Russian (Crude Accountability, July 2025; OC Media; Hetq).

The paper's framing is that these are **structural** vulnerabilities of transit economies — geography, overlapping framework membership (EAEU), regulatory complexity, thin institutional capacity, SMEs without the technical knowledge to assess military relevance — rather than evidence of deliberate state non-compliance. Legislative expansion or punitive enforcement alone is said to be unlikely to fix them and may damage cooperation.

## Institutional Map (for counterparty diligence)

- **Armenia:** State Revenue Committee (customs and tax); Ministry of Economy (dual-use licensing). Covered by the **EU P2P Export Control Programme** global dual-use project (Dec 2023–Dec 2026, Expertise France). 2025 Strategic Agenda for the EU–Armenia Partnership (adopted 2 Dec 2025) provides the regulatory-approximation framework. US–Armenia Strategic Partnership Charter signed 14 Jan 2025.
- **Azerbaijan:** State Customs Committee; Ministry of Economy (strategic goods licensing); Financial Monitoring Service. Covered by the EU-funded CBRN critical-infrastructure protection project (2023–28) via the CBRN Centres of Excellence network. US–Azerbaijan Charter signed 10 Feb 2026 (MoU 8 Aug 2025).
- **Georgia:** the principal regional transit hub, but expressly out of scope for the paper.

Transatlantic coordination caveat: the EU–US Trade and Technology Council has been suspended since the January 2025 change of US administration; coordination now runs informally at embassy level and through Wassenaar working-level channels. The paper's realistic agenda is therefore operational — de-conflicting EU P2P and US EXBS capacity building, typology sharing, and independent EU private-sector outreach with translated toolkits.

---

## Recommendations (addressed to EU policymakers)

1. Position sanctions compliance as a **strategic** element of EU engagement — compliance benchmarks in CFSP country strategies and Eastern Partnership dialogues; EU delegations equipped to communicate expectations to both authorities and companies.
2. Build ACW- and sanctions-related **risk awareness into EU regional planning**, acknowledging the region's transit/re-export role.
3. Support **accessible compliance tools** — deepen EU P2P rather than create parallel instruments; translate guidance and screening resources into Armenian and Azerbaijani; target SMEs, logistics and finance.
4. Pursue **phased, calibrated** alignment focused on highest-risk goods and most exposed transit practices rather than maximalist demands.

## Relevance for UAE / Gulf Compliance Practice

- **Same mechanism, different corridor.** The Art. 12g clause, the anti-circumvention toolkit and the criminalisation directive reach UAE importers of EU goods exactly as they reach Armenian ones. The paper is a usable explainer for UAE counterparties who ask why an EU supplier is demanding re-export undertakings and audit rights.
- **Transit-economy risk profile.** The structural-vulnerability framing (geography, SMEs, customer-supplied documentation in logistics, trade-finance opacity) is a close description of the risk picture regulators apply to Gulf free zones; the Armenian re-export share trajectory is the pattern enforcement analysts look for in trade data.
- **Shipping and energy.** The Azerbaijani-flag tanker designations and the STAR refinery feedstock data illustrate how EU listing risk now attaches to **flag and feedstock**, not only to cargo origin — directly relevant to vessel-screening and charterparty sanctions-clause work. See [Sanctions — Oil & Energy](../regulations/sanctions-oil-energy.md).
- **Confidence-building framing is transferable.** The argument that functioning compliance systems signal governance quality to external partners is the same case made for UAE post-FATF-grey-list positioning.

## Caveats

- Trade-data figures are drawn from press analyses (NYT, WSJ, Sky News, Caucasus Watch) rather than official statistics and are reproduced here as cited.
- The paper is an EU-policy paper with a cooperative-engagement thesis; it does not analyse enforcement cases against named South Caucasus companies and does not cover Georgia.
- Treaty status and bilateral charters are stated as at mid-2026; verify before relying on them.
