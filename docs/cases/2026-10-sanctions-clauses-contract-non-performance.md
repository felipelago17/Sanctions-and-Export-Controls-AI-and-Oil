# When Sanctions Freeze the Contract — BIMCO Clause Limits, Lithuanian Civil-Liability Case Law and Enforcement of Awards (2024–2026)

Three 2026 practitioner analyses, read together, map the contract-law edge of the sanctions regimes covered elsewhere in this repository: what happens when a sanctions measure — or the *risk* of one — makes performance unlawful or commercially intolerable, and who bears the loss.

- **HFW (2026)** reports an unreported London arbitration holding that the **BIMCO Sanctions Clause for Time Charter Parties 2020 does not entitle owners to refuse orders on the basis of a risk of *future* designation** — only where a party or activity is *already* sanctioned. The dispute arose under US Executive Order 14404 (Cuba) and an order to discharge at Mariel.
- **IBA / Motieka & Audzevicius (1 Oct 2026)** reviews Lithuanian Supreme Court and Court of Appeal decisions (2024–2025) holding that **compliance with EU asset-freeze measures is a complete defence to contract claims** — counterparties of a designated or "controlled" entity cannot recover replacement-cost damages, and the designated entity's own receivables are frozen, not extinguished.

- **HFW (14 Jul 2026)** surveys Swiss, EU (CJEU *Reibel*, pending) and English authority on **enforcing judgments and awards in favour of sanctioned creditors** — suspension of the due date, OFSI-licensed payment into blocked accounts, and public-policy review of awards.

All three cut the same way: **the loss lies where it falls unless the contract reallocates it**. Statutory and standard-form protections are narrower than practitioners often assume.

> **Source and verification status:** The London award is unreported; facts and reasoning are as summarised by HFW and cannot be checked against the award. The Lithuanian decisions are identified by case number and are verifiable in the Lithuanian court register; the summary follows the IBA article's unofficial translations. E.O. 14404 and the BIMCO clause text are primary sources.

# Part I — The BIMCO Time Charter Sanctions Clause and Anticipated Sanctions Risk

## Key facts

- **Forum:** London arbitration (LMAA), unreported, 2026
- **Charter:** time trip charter incorporating the **BIMCO Sanctions Clause for Time Charter Parties 2020**
- **Order in dispute:** discharge at **Mariel, Cuba**
- **Sanctions context:** US **Executive Order 14404** (Cuba) — secondary-sanctions exposure for persons operating in sectors of the Cuban economy, owned/controlled by the Government of Cuba, or materially assisting it or designated persons
- **Owners' position:** the consignee and/or port operator might be sanctionable under the E.O.; discharging exposed owners to sanctions risk; refusal justified under sub-clause (e); charterers in breach for failing to give alternative orders within 48 hours under sub-clause (f)
- **Tribunal's holding:** a perception or theoretical risk of **future** designation is **not** a "Sanctioned Party" or "Sanctioned Activity" within the clause; owners' reliance failed

## The clause

The 2020 clause replaced the 2010 Sanctions Clause and the 2013 Designated Entities Clause. Its structure:

| Sub-clause | Content |
|---|---|
| (a) | Definitions: "Sanctioned Party", "Sanctioned Activity", "Sanctioning Authority" |
| (b)–(c) | Mutual warranties that neither party (and, for owners, registered owners, bareboat/disponent owners, managers) is a Sanctioned Party at fixing or during the charter |
| (d) | Termination right on breach of the warranties |
| (e) | **Charterers shall not give orders involving a Sanctioned Party or Sanctioned Activity** |
| (f) | If employment becomes a Sanctioned Activity after commencement, owners may refuse to proceed; charterers must give alternative orders within 48 hours, failing which owners may discharge at any safe port |
| (g)–(h) | Hire continues; charterers indemnify owners for claims arising from compliance |
| (i) | Incorporation into sub-charters and bills of lading |

### The drafting change that decided the case

The **2010** clause allowed owners to refuse employment which, "in the reasonable judgement of the Owners", *would expose* the vessel, owners, managers, crew or insurers to any sanction. That formulation — also reflected in the bespoke clause considered in *The Catalan Sea* — gave owners a wide, judgement-based discretion.

The **2020** clause deliberately removed the "reasonable judgement" test. BIMCO's explanatory notes present the clause as "user friendly and straightforward, without any difficult assessment of potential risk exposure". The price of that simplicity is that the clause is **binary**: it bites only when a party or activity *is* sanctioned, not when it *may become* so. Reed Smith flagged this in February 2020 as a weakening of owners' position; the Mariel award confirms it.

### Why this matters beyond Cuba

HFW's central point is that the gap is systemic, not Cuba-specific. Most US secondary-sanctions programmes — Iran (E.O. 13902, see [Operation Economic Outcast](2026-08-operation-economic-outcast-iran-shipping.md)), Russia (E.O. 14024 and the December 2023 FFI amendments), Venezuela, Cuba — operate by **threat of future designation** of non-US persons who deal with targeted sectors or persons. The exposure is real, often insurable only with difficulty, and commercially decisive — yet under the 2020 BIMCO wording it is not a ground for refusing orders unless the counterparty, port or cargo is already listed.

The problem compounds where beneficial ownership cannot be verified. Cuba has no central corporate registry; HFW notes the same is true of many jurisdictions. Owners may be unable to prove that a consignee *is* a Sanctioned Party even where every indicator suggests state control — and under the 2020 clause, suspicion is not enough.

## Takeaways (HFW, with repository commentary)

- **Do not assume a sanctions clause operates on a risk standard.** Read the trigger: "is a Sanctioned Party" (2020 BIMCO) and "will expose … in the reasonable judgement of Owners" (2010 BIMCO / bespoke) are different contracts.
- **Amend at fixing.** Owners who want protection against secondary-sanctions *risk* should add an express reasonable-judgement limb, a right to refuse orders to named high-risk jurisdictions, or a mechanism for independent sanctions-counsel determination.
- **Use trading limits, not the sanctions clause, to exclude Cuba/Iran/Venezuela/Russia.** Exclusion from trading limits is the clean tool; the sanctions clause is not designed to do that work.
- **Charterers' side:** the award is a useful precedent against owners who invoke generalised sanctions nervousness to escape unprofitable employment. But charterers should expect owners to respond with tighter trading limits and bespoke wording, and should price that in.
- **Insurers / P&I:** the 2020 clause's indemnity (sub-clause (h)) does not reach losses from a refusal the clause did not authorise. Owners who refuse on a risk basis and are found in breach may face off-hire, damages and an uninsured position.

# Part II — Lithuanian Courts on Non-Performance under EU Asset Freezes

## Legal framework

| Instrument | Provision | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Council Regulation (EU) No 269/2014 | Art. 2 | Freeze of funds and economic resources of listed persons and of entities owned or controlled by them |
| Regulation 269/2014 | Art. 11(1) | "No claims" clause — no satisfaction of claims in connection with contracts whose performance is affected by the measures, where brought by designated persons or persons acting through them |
| Lithuanian Law on International Sanctions | Art. 7(3) | Pre-existing contracts **must** be terminated or suspended for the duration of the sanctions |
| Lithuanian Law on International Sanctions | Art. 7(4) | **No civil liability** for non-performance resulting from implementation of international sanctions |

## The cases

### 1. *UAB Vydmantai wind park v UAB Elektrum Lietuva* — Supreme Court of Lithuania, 27 June 2024, No e3K-3-139-701/2024

A 30 MW wind farm, treated as controlled by a designated person, sued its electricity off-taker for payment for power already delivered, plus penalties. **Held:** the receivable is itself a frozen "fund or economic resource"; the claim cannot be enforced while the measures apply. The debt is **not extinguished** — it may be pursued once the measures are lifted.

*Significance:* the asset freeze reaches the designated entity's *contractual claims*, not only its bank balances. A counterparty that withholds payment to a controlled entity is not in breach; a court will not give judgment for the controlled entity. This is the EU mirror of the OFAC blocked-account logic discussed in the [Lukoil case study](2026-11-lukoil-boehly-securities-swap-ofac.md) — value owed to a sanctioned person is immobilised, not cancelled.

### 2. *AB ORLEN Lietuva v AB Inter RAO Lietuva* — Court of Appeal of Lithuania, 12 March 2025, No e2A-39-881/2025

Inter RAO Lietuva, a major electricity supplier, was found by the FNTT (Lithuanian financial-crime service) to be controlled by a designated person. Nord Pool suspended its trading; the regulator suspended and then revoked its supply permit. From May 2022 it could not perform. ORLEN bought replacement power in a sharply risen market and claimed c. **EUR 7.93 m** plus interest. **Dismissed in full.**

*Reasoning:* Art. 7(4) is a **lex specialis** that bars civil liability outright for sanctions-driven non-performance, displacing the Civil Code force majeure regime. Suspension was the *only lawful course*, not a permitted option; no unlawful act, therefore no liability threshold crossed. The buyer was equally bound by the statutory duty, so the immunity protects **all parties** to the transaction, not only the sanctioned one.

### 3. *AB Roquette Amilina v AB Inter RAO Lietuva* — Court of Appeal of Lithuania, 27 March 2025, No e2A-75-467/2025

Same supplier, same facts; claim of c. **EUR 6.11 m**. **Dismissed in full** — but by a different route. This panel read Art. 7(4) as requiring the defendant to satisfy the **ordinary force majeure conditions** (unforeseeability, unavoidability, causation), which it found met, locating the force majeure event in **Russia's war against Ukraine** rather than in the regulators' decisions.

### 4. Supreme Court selection panel, 1 July 2025, procedural No 2-55-3-01399-2023-4

Refused to accept ORLEN's cassation appeal and its request for a **CJEU preliminary reference** on Art. 11(1) of Regulation 269/2014, on the basis that the provision would not affect the outcome. The split between the two Court of Appeal panels on the *mechanism* (statutory immunity vs. force majeure) therefore stands unresolved at the apex level.

## Doctrinal significance

1. **Two routes to the same result — with different reach.** The *ORLEN* lex-specialis reading makes compliance a complete defence regardless of foreseeability; the *Amilina* force-majeure reading leaves open an argument that sanctions risk was foreseeable (e.g. for contracts concluded after February 2022 with Russia-linked counterparties) and therefore not excused. Which reading other Member State courts adopt will determine whether post-2022 contracts with later-designated parties carry recoverable damages.

2. **"Controlled by" is where the risk sits.** Neither Inter RAO Lietuva nor the wind farm was itself listed; both were treated as *controlled* by a designated person under Art. 2 of Regulation 269/2014. The EU control test (not a bright-line 50%) is the analytical hinge, and the FNTT's administrative finding was decisive. Compare [OFAC UBO & 50% Rule](../regulations/ofac-ubo-50-percent-rule.md).

3. **Art. 11 "no claims" clause not engaged.** The courts decided on national law and did not need Art. 11(1). The Supreme Court's refusal to refer means the scope of the EU-level no-claims bar — in particular whether it protects *non-designated* parties who comply — remains for another court.

4. **Market risk is reallocated to the buyer.** The commercial lesson of the energy cases is that a buyer whose supplier is later found to be sanctions-controlled bears the full replacement-cost exposure — here, EUR 14 m across two claimants in a spiking 2022 power market — with no statutory recourse.

## Takeaways

- **Contract is the only protection.** Sanctions-triggered suspension/termination rights, price-adjustment or pass-through mechanisms that survive a counterparty's designation, step-in and replacement-supply rights, and security or escrow for pre-paid value.
- **Diligence the control chain, not the list.** Both Lithuanian defendants were unlisted; the exposure came from control findings. Supplier onboarding should include a documented control analysis under the EU "ownership or control" test and the OFAC 50% Rule.
- **Diversify concentrated exposure** to counterparties with Russia/Belarus ownership links; the courts will not socialise the loss.
- **For designated-side receivables:** a frozen claim survives; keep the paper trail, interrupt limitation where national law permits, and plan for recovery on delisting.
- **For UAE / third-country counterparties of EU entities:** expect EU suppliers to invoke statutory immunity for sanctions-driven non-performance; governing-law and forum choices now materially affect whether damages are recoverable at all.

# Part III — Enforcing Judgments and Awards For or Against Sanctioned Parties

HFW's 14 July 2026 briefing, *Sanctions and commodities: resisting payment of a judgment debt to a sanctioned entity*, completes the picture: even where a sanctioned (or sanctions-controlled) creditor *wins*, enforcement may be suspended, licensed into a blocked account, or reopened on public-policy grounds.

## The cases

| Case | Court / status | Regime | Holding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4A_305/2025** (Angolan diamonds/minerals company, controlled by an Annex 8-listed entity, enforcing an LCIA award) | Swiss Federal Tribunal, 2025 | Swiss Ordinance 946.231.176.721, Art. 15 | Art. 15(2) applies to enforcement of an award or judgment; the debtor cannot be compelled to make a prohibited payment, and the blocked-account exceptions (Art. 15(2ter), (2quater)) do not permit enforcement through state mechanisms. **Due date suspended for the duration of sanctions; interest stops accruing; obligation revives on delisting.** The lower court had gone further (claim lapsed through objective impossibility, Swiss CO Art. 119(1)); the Federal Tribunal preferred suspension. |
| **C-802/24, *NV Reibel v JSC VO Stankoimport*** | CJEU reference from Svea Court of Appeal; AG Biondi opinion 26 Feb 2026; judgment pending | Reg. 833/2014, **Art. 11** (no-claims clause) | Russian buyer made an advance part-payment for dual-use goods whose export Belgium then blocked; Reibel kept the advance; Stankoimport won an award for its return. **AG:** Art. 11 is EU public policy; a national court hearing a set-aside application has the right, and arguably the duty, to review *ex officio* whether the tribunal applied Art. 11 correctly and to set the award aside if not. |
| ***PJSC National Bank Trust v Mints*** [2023] EWHC 118 (Comm); [2023] EWCA Civ 1332 | English Commercial Court / Court of Appeal | UK Russia Regulations 2019 | Payment of a judgment debt to a designated person **can be licensed by OFSI**; a judgment in favour of a sanctioned claimant is not a nullity, though funds will in practice go to a blocked account. |
| ***Celestial Aviation*** [2026] UKSC 10 | UK Supreme Court | SAMLA 2018, s. 44 | Suggests that s. 44 (protection for persons who reasonably believed they were complying with sanctions) may form part of English public policy — opening an argument that an English court could refuse to enforce a foreign award or judgment inconsistent with it. |

## Doctrinal significance

1. **Suspension, not extinction — mostly.** Switzerland (Federal Tribunal) and Lithuania (*Vydmantai*, Part II) converge: the sanctioned party's claim is immobilised, interest may stop, and the claim revives on delisting. But the Swiss lower court's "objective impossibility" route and HFW's own observation that a multi-year freeze could *extinguish* the debt show the outcome is not settled, and may turn on national limitation and impossibility rules.

2. **Public policy as a second bite at sanctions issues.** If the CJEU follows AG Biondi, Art. 11 of Reg. 833/2014 becomes a ground on which EU courts must *re-examine* arbitral awards at set-aside or enforcement — regardless of whether the tribunal considered it. The same logic, via SAMLA s. 44, may be emerging in England. Arbitration's finality is being qualified by sanctions law.

3. **The Reibel facts matter for energy and technology contracts.** Advance payments for dual-use or controlled equipment that is then blocked by an export-licensing decision are a recurring pattern in oil-and-gas procurement from Russia-linked buyers. Whether the seller may keep the advance (Art. 11) or must return it is precisely the question pending in Luxembourg — see [EAR Interaction Risk Matrix](../regulations/ear-interaction-risk-matrix.md) and the [M&A diligence manual](../enforcements/ma-export-controls-diligence.md) for the export-control side of the same transactions.

4. **Jurisdictional arbitrage.** The UK (licensable payment into a blocked account), Switzerland (suspension of due date) and the EU (potential public-policy set-aside) now offer materially different outcomes for the same award. Choice of seat and enforcement venue is a sanctions-risk decision.

## Takeaways

**Creditors (traders, suppliers)** — check your own group's control chain before suing: a control finding against a parent converts a winning award into a suspended one. Expect delay; in Switzerland, expect no interest. In the UK, map the OFSI licensing route early.

**Judgment debtors** — raise sanctions at the enforcement stage, expressly on public-policy grounds in the EU and Switzerland; in England, consider SAMLA s. 44 reasonable-belief arguments. Monitor *Reibel*.

**Both** — treat *Reibel* as live: if the CJEU adopts the AG's view, every EU-seated award touching a Russia-related contract becomes reviewable for Art. 11 compliance.

## Cross-regime synthesis

| Question | BIMCO 2020 (English law arbitration) | Lithuanian courts (EU law) |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger for lawful non-performance | Party/activity **already** sanctioned | Measures **in force** and performance thereby unlawful |
| Anticipated / risk-based exposure | Not covered | Not addressed; force-majeure route may exclude foreseeable risk |
| Who bears the loss | Party that refuses without a clause trigger is in breach | Loss lies where it falls; statutory immunity for all parties |
| Remedy for the frozen party | — | Receivable survives; enforceable on delisting |
| Practical fix | Amend clause / trading limits at fixing | Bespoke suspension, pricing and security terms |

The common thread across the [Lukoil](2026-11-lukoil-boehly-securities-swap-ofac.md), [Operation Economic Outcast](2026-08-operation-economic-outcast-iran-shipping.md) and these contract-law cases is that sanctions regimes are increasingly designed around **the threat of future designation** of non-targeted persons — secondary sanctions, control tests, good-faith revocation clauses — while standard-form contracts and national civil-liability rules still assume a **binary listed / not-listed world**. The gap is where counsel earns its fee.
