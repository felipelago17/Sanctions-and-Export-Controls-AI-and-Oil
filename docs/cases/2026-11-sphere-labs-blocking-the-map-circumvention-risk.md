# Sphere Labs: "Blocking the Map Isn't Enough" — Circumvention Risk and Payment Flows (Nov 2026)

**Summary.** A payments-compliance viewpoint piece argues that jurisdiction-only screening misses export-control circumvention, and that payment flows can surface diversion earlier than customs records. It uses the Megaspeed matter as its case.

> **Source and verification status.** Author: Scott F. Butler, Chief Compliance Officer, Sphere Labs/SpherePay (spherepay.co blog, c. 24 Nov 2026). **Vendor viewpoint (Tier 3).** The text provided was a digest of the article, not the full original; wording and some details may differ. Facts about Megaspeed are cross-checked in the [Blackwell case study](2026-11-blackwell-chips-in-china-despite-us-curbs.md) and the [joint-statement note](2026-11-commerce-doj-megaspeed-joint-statement.md). Nothing is proven against any party.

## What the article argues

| Point | Summary |
|---|---|
| Core thesis | "Block the map" (country lists) misses circumvention that runs through intermediaries and newly reorganised entities |
| Megaspeed pattern | Singapore data-centre firm, formerly 7Road International under a Chinese parent; ownership change in 2023; later an Nvidia Cloud Partner; over $4bn of Nvidia hardware imported; reported ~$800m further controlled technology to its Malaysian subsidiary after Nvidia's June 2025 no-sell listing (trade records, unverified); Commerce probe since 2024 |
| Three channels | (1) redomiciling ("Singapore washing"); (2) intermediaries: resellers, integrators, false declarations, re-exports; (3) remote access, where the 16 Nov 2026 BIS rule requires licences for advanced chips shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil |
| Scale estimate | Epoch AI estimate cited: chips equal to ~1.6m H100s smuggled to China through 2025 (not verified here) |
| Payment red flags | Recurring lease or subscription payments; financing draws exceeding installed capacity; payer and end-user mismatch; sudden activity at newly reorganised operators. FinCEN/BIS joint red flags cover money as well as goods |
| Vendor model | "Sanctions Globe": lane risk scored in three layers: jurisdiction baseline, channel overlay (known corridors), counterparty overlay (verified "Trusted Trade Channel" participants lower risk). Warns of under- and over-blocking |

## Entity List action (outside the article's own sourcing)

Secondary reports say BIS added Megaspeed and six linked entities (Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand) to the Entity List by final rule (document 2026-23999; public inspection 23 Nov, publication scheduled 25 Nov 2026): licence required for all EAR items, presumption of denial, 30-day savings clause. Federal Register text not read. Nvidia reportedly created a board-level national security and export control committee; Megaspeed says it cooperates and denies wrongdoing.

## Why it matters

- It frames financial-flow monitoring as a diversion control, consistent with the financing-trace evidence in the Blackwell case.
- It separates the three channels a screening programme must cover: ownership change, intermediaries and remote access.

## Relevance to UAE and Gulf compliance functions

- Country-based screening is insufficient for hubs; ownership history, aliases (e.g. a former name) and recent reorganisations belong in onboarding and periodic review.
- Payment-side signals (payer versus end-user mismatch, financing ahead of capacity) are usable by banks, lessors and procurement teams, including for cloud and GPU leasing.
- The BIS remote-access rule's country list does not include the UAE; treat the list as moving.

## Contested and unverified items

- The $800m post-listing figure, the Epoch AI estimate and the $4bn import figure are the article's relayed claims.
- The "Sanctions Globe" model is a commercial product; effectiveness is not independently evidenced.
- The article's recommendations are vendor positions, not regulatory requirements.
