# ICC Netherlands — Business Integrity Commission: Minimum Standards for Companies on Sanctions Compliance (2026)

**Source:** ICC Netherlands, Business Integrity Commission, Working Group on Sanctions, *Minimum standards for companies on sanctions compliance*, 18 pp. (2026; cites the Commission's consolidated FAQs of 6 May 2026 and UK guidance of 22 April 2026). Published at iccwbo.nl.

**Why it is here:** a short, practitioner-written statement of what trading and manufacturing companies in an EU jurisdiction are now expected to have in place — written explicitly from the perspective of how **banks, auditors, insurers and supervisors** will assess the company. It is the EU counterpart to OFAC's *Framework for Compliance Commitments* and the BIS *Export Compliance Program* elements, and a useful benchmark for UAE companies whose EU suppliers and correspondent banks apply exactly these expectations to them.

---

## Framing

The Working Group's starting point is that list screening alone is no longer compliance. Since the EU criminalisation directive (Directive 2024/1226, adopted 12 April 2024) violation *and circumvention* are criminal offences with minimum penalty rules, and liability attaches where a company **knew or should have understood** the risk and accepted it. Companies must therefore apply risk-based due diligence to end use, end destination, counterparties and trade structures, and **record their reasoning demonstrably**. Banks sit as gatekeepers translating these obligations into their own risk frameworks — including third-country (US, UK) extraterritorial regimes — so a company that cannot explain its controls will find transactions delayed, refused or blocked even where no sanction formally applies.

---

## Core principles

1. **Risk-based action** — tailored to nature, size and geographic exposure, including assets, activities and counterparties, not just the company's own location.
2. **Auditability** — choices and considerations documented and explainable.
3. **Circumvention as a stand-alone risk** — indirect routes addressed, not only direct designations.
4. **Stakeholder interaction** — the company understands its own exposure and can explain its controls to banks, auditors, insurers and customers.
5. **Dynamism** — the programme adjusts with each package.

## Minimum elements

### Sanctions risk assessment (documented, periodically updated)
Covers relevant regimes (NL, EU, UN and, where material, US and UK); the national ML/TF risk assessment insofar as it concerns targeted financial sanctions; production, transit and destination countries; nature of goods including dual-use or military characteristics; use of agents, distributors, brokers; exposure to high-risk countries and circumvention routes. Financial institutions under the Dutch Sanctions Act 1977 additionally need AO/IC measures to detect and freeze relationships with listed persons and to report hits immediately to the supervisor.

### Governance
Formal sanctions policy; allocated responsibilities; documented escalation paths; periodic **training** proportionate to role, exposure and line of defence — treated as a fundamental element alongside management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls and testing/audit. In the Netherlands an internal compliance programme is in some cases mandatory for licence and exemption applications.

### Ownership due diligence
EU ownership-and-control rule: an entity **50 % or more** owned, directly or indirectly, by a listed person — or otherwise controlled — is captured even if unlisted. Screening must therefore reach UBO and control structures on a risk basis, with enhanced due diligence and documentation for higher-risk transactions, complex structures and opaque registers. Screening tools should support ownership-chain analysis.

### Supply-chain due diligence
Companies are expected to prevent, demonstrably, direct or indirect contribution to violation or circumvention; identify, mitigate and where necessary escalate or terminate circumvention risk in the value chain.

### Circumvention
The Russia regime lowered the knowledge threshold: participation in an activity that circumvents sanctions is prohibited where the person **is aware that participation may have that object or effect and accepts that possibility** — no intent required. Expected of companies: familiarity with typologies (third-country diversion, end-user switching, sham intermediaries); sector-specific red flags; documented escalation and decision processes; and the ability to show **why a transaction was accepted despite indirect risk**. Quality of analysis, proportionality and documentation are what banks and prosecutors assess.

### Contractual provisions
The Art. 12g **no-re-export-to-Russia/Belarus clause** must create enforceable liability under the governing law; a best-efforts formulation or a general compliance warranty is **not sufficient**. Clauses to be documented and reviewed periodically. (Compare the EUNPDC analysis of the same mechanism reaching South Caucasus importers.)

### Screening tools
Risk-appropriate tooling covering the relevant lists, aliases and ownership; stale list data or untuned ownership screening produces false positives as readily as true hits.

### Record-keeping and handling results
Retain the evidence of screening, decisions and rationale; defined procedures for true matches (freeze/refuse/report) and for documented clearance of false positives.

### Relationship with banks and other stakeholders
Expect to be asked for the risk assessment, policy, screening evidence and transaction rationale; a constructive, prepared dialogue reduces blocked payments.

## "When has a company done enough?"

The Working Group declines to set a hard standard, but says a company has done enough where it has **(a)** identified the relevant sanctions risks, **(b)** explicitly included circumvention in the analysis, **(c)** taken appropriate, proportionate measures, and **(d)** can substantiate and document its decisions. Absent these, banks and counterparties will take a conservative line even where no sanction formally applies, and law-enforcement authorities will weigh the robustness of the programme in any criminal investigation.
